# Core Copywriting Principles

These apply to every format, every audience, every product. Read before writing anything.

---

## 1. The Rule of One (Wiebe)

Before writing anything, define six singularities. Copy that tries to serve more than one of any of these dilutes all of them.

- **One reader** — the specific person you're writing to. Know their pain, goal, and objections. Not a segment; one person.
- **One offer** — one thing being sold or asked for, with no competing asks alongside it.
- **One promise** — the single measurable outcome the reader can expect. From all the benefits you could name, pick the strongest one.
- **One transformation** — the belief shift the reader must make to get from skepticism to yes. Map the from/to: "From: I can't do this on my own → To: The right framework changes that." Address these beliefs before presenting the offer.
- **One CTA** — one action, clearly stated. Frame it as a call to value, not a call to click: "Start publishing videos that open doors" not "Sign up."
- **One voice** — consistent personality throughout. Tone can vary; voice doesn't.

**Bad:** "Whether you're a solo founder, a growing team, or an enterprise — we have solutions for every stage. Get a demo, start a free trial, or talk to sales."
*(Three readers. Three CTAs. No promise. No transformation.)*

**Good:** "For freelancers tired of chasing invoices: get paid in two days or less. [Start your free trial]"
*(One reader. One promise. One CTA.)*

When you have real customer language — the exact words someone used to describe their pain or desire — use it verbatim. If a customer wrote "I was drowning in invoices every Sunday night," that's a headline. The language that carries the most emotional weight is always the language the reader already uses.

## 2. Enter at the Right Stage of Awareness (Schwartz)

The reader's current awareness level determines what you lead with. Writing to the wrong stage is one of the most common copy failures — pitching to someone who doesn't know they have a problem, or over-explaining to someone ready to buy.

- **Unaware** — doesn't know a problem exists. Lead with a story, observation, or provocative truth that names something they recognize but haven't articulated. Never mention your product yet.
- **Problem-aware** — knows the pain, doesn't know solutions exist. Lead with their exact struggle. Show you understand what they're experiencing before offering anything.
- **Solution-aware** — knows solutions exist, hasn't chosen one. Lead with your mechanism — what makes your approach different from what they've already considered or tried.
- **Product-aware** — knows your product, hasn't committed. Lead with objection handling, proof, and value stacking. Address the hesitation that's in the way.
- **Most aware** — ready to buy. Lead with the offer and make it easy to say yes. Don't over-educate; they don't need convincing, they need a clear path forward.

## 3. Specificity Is the Engine of Believability (Caples, Hopkins, Ogilvy)

Specific claims are more believable than general ones. This isn't stylistic — it's been tested repeatedly. Concrete details, numbers, and named outcomes signal that a real person verified a real result. Vague claims signal the opposite.

Superlatives — "best," "revolutionary," "amazing," "lowest prices" — actively damage credibility. Claude Hopkins: they suggest "looseness of expression, a tendency to exaggerate, a carelessness of truth," and lead readers to discount everything else you say. Replace every superlative with the specific fact that would justify it.

- Weak: "Our prices have been reduced." → Strong: "Our prices have been reduced 25 per cent."
- Weak: "Visit America for less than you suppose." → Strong: "Tour America for 35 pounds a week."
- Weak: "You'll have more freedom." → Strong: "Take a 1pm movie break on a Wednesday without asking anyone's permission."

Numbers, quantities, timeframes, names, and sensory details all do this work. The more precisely you describe the outcome or the proof, the more believable it becomes.

## 4. Emotions First, Logic Second

Copy must make the reader feel something before it makes a logical case. There are two sides to this: the pain state and the dream state. Work both.

**Naming the pain state:** Don't describe the problem abstractly — describe the specific moment the reader is living in. Not "cash flow stress" but "lying awake at 2am running the same numbers again." The more precisely you name it, the more the reader feels understood. Then agitate: show what happens if the problem continues. Don't resolve the tension early.

**Painting the dream state:** Once pain is established, make the desired outcome vivid and sensory. Not "more freedom" but "taking a 1pm movie break on a Wednesday without asking anyone's permission." The reader should be able to see, hear, and feel the outcome before they've bought anything.

**Emotional contrast:** The gap between the pain state and the dream state is the engine. The wider and more vivid that gap, the stronger the pull toward the offer.

**Target high-activation emotions** (Berger): joy, awe, anger, humor, curiosity. These drive action. Sadness doesn't — it's low-activation and makes people stop, not move.

Logic follows emotion. Once the reader feels something, give them the rational reasons to act on it.

---

## 5. Concrete Imagery Over Abstract Claims

Humans think in sensory details and specific scenes, not abstractions. Abstract claims ask the reader to do imaginative work on your behalf; concrete imagery does it for them. Every abstract claim has a concrete version that's more credible and more memorable.

- **Specific names and numbers:** Not "a local business attracted more customers" but "Jenny Lee's bakery went from 60 customers a day to 314 — she's thinking of removing the entry bell because the constant dinging is unbearable."
- **Sensory details:** Engage sight, sound, smell, taste, touch. "The smell of leather when you open the door" lands differently than "premium interior."
- **Color and texture words:** Anchor abstract ideas to physical reality. Specific colors and textures stick in memory where adjectives like "luxurious" don't.
- **People and problems over product specs:** Details about a real person solving a real problem are more interesting and believable than feature descriptions alone.

The test: can the reader picture it? If not, it's still abstract.

## 6. Communicate Value in Terms the Reader Already Cares About

Value is perceived, not inherent. The same product can feel worth nothing or worth everything depending on how it's framed. Hormozi's value equation makes this concrete:

**Value = (Dream Outcome × Perceived Likelihood) ÷ (Time Delay × Effort & Sacrifice)**

Work all four levers in copy:
- **Dream outcome** — make the transformation as vivid and specific as possible. What does winning actually look like for this reader?
- **Perceived likelihood** — close the credibility gap. Proof, case studies, step-by-step systems, and guarantees all increase the reader's belief that *they specifically* can get this result.
- **Time delay** — the faster the result, the more valuable the offer feels. "In 10 days" beats "over time."
- **Effort and sacrifice** — the less the reader has to do, the more valuable it feels. Most copy ignores this lever entirely.

Most copy addresses the dream outcome and stops there. The bottom of the equation — time and effort — is where underexplored value lives.

Don't stop at benefits. Go to outcomes in the reader's own language:
- **Feature:** "Automated invoice reminders"
- **Benefit:** "Spend less time chasing payments"
- **Outcome:** "Stop dreading Monday mornings"

## 7. The Slippery Slide (Sugarman / Wiebe)

The sole purpose of the headline is to get the reader to read the first sentence. The sole purpose of the first sentence is to get the reader to read the second. Every element exists to pull the reader to the next one. Sugarman called this the slippery slide. The technique that creates it is the open loop.

**How to open a loop:** Raise a question, start a story without finishing it, or promise something you haven't revealed yet. The reader's mind is compelled to close loops — they'll keep reading to resolve the tension. Examples:
- Subject line: "That time I almost lost the client" — the reader needs to know what happened
- Mid-copy: "But here's where it gets strange." — a loop that makes the next paragraph unavoidable
- End of section: "Let me explain why that matters." — delays resolution to keep forward motion

**Specific techniques:**
- **Short first sentence.** So easy to read the reader is already in motion before deciding to engage.
- **Seeds of curiosity** (Sugarman): end paragraphs in ways that open rather than close: "But there's more to it than that." "Here's where it gets interesting."
- **Never resolve tension early.** Don't deliver the payoff before the reader has been pulled through the body.
- **One idea per sentence.** Compound sentences slow momentum.

Always close the loop eventually. Loops that never resolve become clickbait and destroy trust.

Diagnostic: read the copy and ask where a real reader would stop. That's where to rewrite.

## 8. Handle Objections Before They Arise (Halbert, Sugarman)

Every reader is running a quiet inner monologue of doubts while reading. If those doubts go unaddressed, they accumulate into resistance. If you name them first, the reader feels understood rather than sold to.

Raise the objection yourself before the reader forms it consciously. The reader who sees their hesitation acknowledged and dissolved keeps moving. The reader who has to raise it themselves stops.

**The five universal objections** every piece of copy faces in some form:
- **No need** — "This isn't my problem"
- **No trust** — "Why should I believe this?"
- **No money** — "I can't afford this" / "It's not worth it"
- **No desire** — "I don't want this enough to act"
- **No urgency** — "I'll think about it later"

**Techniques:**
- **"Even if" phrasing:** Acknowledge the specific barrier directly. "Even if you've never written a word of copy." "Even if you've tried three other tools and they didn't work."
- **Raise then resolve:** State the objection plainly, then immediately dissolve it with proof, a reframe, or a guarantee. Nest: "Most customers installed it in 30 minutes or less" — preempts the installation fear before it forms.
- **Price reframing:** Don't wait for sticker shock. Contextualize the price against the cost of the problem or the value of the outcome before the number appears.

Build a complete list of every hesitation the reader might have, then work through them in copy before they're asked.

## 9. Truth, Fascinatingly Told (Ogilvy)

The goal is never to embellish or exaggerate — it's to research deeply enough that the honest truth becomes the most compelling thing you could say.

Ogilvy spent weeks studying products before writing a word. For Rolls-Royce, he found one engineering fact: at 60 miles an hour, the loudest noise in the car comes from the electric clock. That single true detail — presented plainly, without decoration — became the headline and sold more cars than any superlative could. The fact *was* the fascination.

- **Research until you find the fact that does the selling.** There is a detail about every product that, stated plainly, makes the right reader stop. It's almost never the obvious claim. It's the specific, unexpected, true thing most writers walk past.
- **All facts, no adjectives.** Replace "luxurious," "revolutionary," "best-in-class" with the specific fact that would justify the adjective. If you can't find that fact, the adjective shouldn't be there.
- **The more you tell, the more you sell.** Factual, specific copy outperforms vague puffery. Long copy dense with verifiable facts consistently outperforms short copy that makes claims without proof.
- **Never write anything you couldn't defend as true.** If the claim requires hedging, exaggerating, or hoping the reader won't check — rewrite it.

## What good looks like

The principles above don't operate one at a time. The examples below show real copy annotated across multiple principles simultaneously — to show how they interact when working, and what compound failure looks like when they don't.

---

### Multiple principles in combination: Dollar Shave Club launch video (2012)

> "Hi, I'm Mike, founder of DollarShaveClub.com. What is DollarShaveClub.com? Well, for a dollar a month we send high-quality razors right to your door."
>
> "Are the blades any good? No. Our blades are f***ing great."
>
> "Do you think your razor needs a vibrating handle, a flashlight, a backscratcher, and ten blades? Your handsome-ass grandfather had one blade and polio."
>
> "Stop paying for shave tech you don't need."

**Rule of One (#1):** One reader — a man who shaves and quietly resents paying $19 for a razor. One offer — $1/month, razors by mail. One promise — stop overpaying for technology you didn't ask for. The video never widens the aperture to reach a broader audience and never muddles the ask.

**Emotions before logic (#4):** The first laugh — "Our blades are f***ing great" — lands before a single specification is named. Relief, recognition, and humor are established in the first 15 seconds. The actual product details (stainless steel blades, aloe vera lubrication strip) come later, once the viewer is already emotionally committed.

**Specificity (#3):** "A dollar a month" — not "affordable," not "budget-friendly." A number that requires no interpretation. "Your handsome-ass grandfather had one blade and polio" is equally specific: not "simpler times" but a named object and a named disease. The specificity is what makes both lines land rather than slide past.

**Slippery slide (#7):** Each setup-and-punchline pair is structured so the reveal creates forward momentum into the next beat. "Are the blades any good? No. Our blades are f***ing great." opens a loop, closes it, and leaves the viewer waiting to see where the next one goes. Nothing resolves completely — the energy keeps moving.

The video cost approximately $4,500 to produce. It drove 12,000 orders in the first two days and crashed the company's servers. Dollar Shave Club was acquired by Unilever in 2016 for $1 billion.

### Principle 2 in practice: the awareness stage mismatch

The most common copy failure is not bad writing — it's the right argument aimed at the wrong stage.

**Weak — solution-aware copy aimed at an unaware audience:**

> "Compare our three pricing tiers. The Pro plan includes unlimited exports, API access, and priority support."

A reader who has never considered whether this category of software could help them has no frame for "API access" or "unlimited exports." The copy assumes they're already evaluating options. They're not. They don't know they have a problem, which means every feature listed registers as noise.

**Strong — entering at the unaware stage:**

> "Most sales teams track their pipeline in spreadsheets. They also lose about 30% of their leads to follow-up gaps. Those two things are not a coincidence."

This doesn't name a product, doesn't ask anyone to compare plans, doesn't name a category. It names a behavior the reader recognizes ("we use spreadsheets") and attaches a consequence they didn't know was connected to it. The problem is introduced before the solution category is named. An unaware reader encounters it as insight, not as a pitch.

The principle: every awareness stage has its own entry point. For unaware readers, behavior comes first, consequence second, problem named last. The product never appears until the reader has felt the problem. Write for where the reader actually is — not where you need them to be.

---

### Principle 4 in practice: emotions first in B2B

This principle is violated most reliably in B2B software, where the reflex is to demonstrate capability before establishing resonance.

**Weak — logic-first:**

> "Our platform integrates with 200+ tools, automates reporting, and gives your team real-time visibility into every project."

A features list with no emotional entry point. The reader can't feel it. Every competing product says something structurally identical. There is nothing to grab onto.

**Strong — emotions first:**

> "Wrestling with projects? It doesn't have to be this hard."
> — Basecamp

The reader who has lived through a chaotic project sprint recognizes this immediately. The emotion — exhaustion, quiet frustration — is named before any feature is introduced. "It doesn't have to be this hard" is the turn from pain to possibility, delivered in eight words. The product's capabilities follow. But the reader is already leaning forward, asking what makes it easier — rather than asking whether they need it.

The distinction: when logic leads, the reader evaluates the product against competitors. When emotion leads, the reader evaluates whether they want the problem solved. Those are different decisions, and the second one is far easier to make.

---

### What compound failure looks like

When multiple principles fail at once, the copy doesn't just underperform — it actively repels the right reader.

**Compound failure:**

> "We're excited to announce the launch of our revolutionary new productivity suite, designed to help businesses of all sizes achieve their goals faster. Whether you're a solo freelancer or a Fortune 500 enterprise, our platform has solutions for everyone. Sign up today and start your free trial!"

Principle violations, counted:

1. **Rule of One:** three readers (solo freelancer, small business, Fortune 500), no single promise, no single transformation — "achieve their goals" means nothing.
2. **Awareness stage:** no entry point — this copy assumes the reader knows what the product does and has already decided to try it.
3. **Specificity:** "revolutionary," "productivity suite," "achieve their goals faster" — every word is a superlative or abstraction. Nothing is concrete enough to be believed.
4. **Emotions:** "We're excited to announce" centers the brand's feeling, not the reader's situation.
5. **Truth:** "Revolutionary" is undefended. "Businesses of all sizes" is a claim of breadth that signals the copy is written for no one in particular.

No single violation makes copy fatal. All five together produce the copy equivalent of trying to shake hands with a fist — the motion is there, but nothing connects.
