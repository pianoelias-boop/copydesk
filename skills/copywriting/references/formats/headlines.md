# Headlines & Taglines

The headline is the single highest-leverage sentence in any piece of copy. Ogilvy: five times as many people read the headline as read the body copy.

---

## The Three Keys to Headlines

Every strong headline draws from one or more of these three sources:

**1. Pains** — Name a specific frustration the reader is experiencing. The more precisely you name it, the more the reader feels understood. Pain-based headlines earn attention because they reflect the reader's inner monologue back to them.

**2. Beliefs** — People act from the beliefs they hold about themselves and the world. The most potent beliefs are the ones people hold but rarely say aloud. A headline that surfaces and validates an unspoken belief creates instant recognition. See the beliefs reference list below.

**3. Specifics** — Details add believability. "3 egg whites, 6 almonds, 4 cashews, 2 dates, no B.S." is more credible than "a simple breakfast." "At 60 miles an hour the loudest noise in this new Rolls-Royce comes from the electric clock" proves the claim through precision. The specific detail does the convincing that a vague promise never can.

---

## Pain Points by Stage of Awareness

How you address pain depends entirely on where the reader is in their awareness:

- **Unaware:** Talk about what people like them do — don't name the problem yet; enter through a relatable behavior or situation
- **Pain Aware:** Name the pain directly; the reader should feel seen
- **Solution Aware:** Talk about options they've already tried, or why the obvious solutions haven't worked
- **Product Aware:** Lead with your product's specific value proposition
- **Most Aware:** Lead with the offer — features, pricing, the decision they need to make

---

## Curiosity

Curiosity creates the pull that makes readers continue. It works through five mechanisms:

1. **Questions** — the mind reflexively reaches for the answer
2. **Unfinished stories** — narrative tension that needs resolution
3. **Unexpected information** — something that violates a held assumption
4. **Implying you have information they don't** — positions the copy as the source of something valuable
5. **Implying they've forgotten something they already know** — "the mistake you're probably making right now"

Curiosity alone isn't sufficient. A headline that opens a loop but offers no credible promise of a worthwhile payoff loses the reader. Curiosity earns the click; the promise justifies it.

---

## The Promise: Making It Believable

A great headline promise has two components: a pain point and enough believability to make the reader think "this could actually work."

Scammy-sounding promises — "Lose 30 pounds in 7 days" — have been so thoroughly overused that they trigger skepticism rather than desire. The credibility of the promise is now a precondition for its persuasiveness. Make the claim specific, ground it in something real, and don't overclaim.

---

## Emotions: High-Activation vs. Low-Activation

Emotions that drive action are high-activation: joy, humor, anger, awe, disgust. These create energy and forward movement.

Low-activation emotions — particularly sadness — don't drive action. Sadness is passive; it tends to make people stop rather than move.

When a headline targets an emotional state, target one that moves. An angry or awe-struck reader acts. A sad reader doesn't.

## The Beliefs Reference List (CopyHackers)

These are the underlying beliefs people carry. A headline that touches one of these creates recognition:

The world is against me / It's us against them / It's not my fault / There must be someone to blame / They're lying to us / It's too late / I'm not in control / I'm not worthy / Others know a secret I don't / If I could just get X, life would get better / If only they knew / There must be an easier way / Life is dangerous / The threat is real / Difference is dangerous / A fool and his money are soon parted / Others have had all the breaks / I don't deserve X / There's only so much X to go around / I'll never be good enough / I'm a fraud / It's only a matter of time / I need permission / There's always tomorrow / I'm the exception / More is better

Example in action: "Shouldn't it be easier than this?" — activates "there must be an easier way."

---

## Subheads

Every subhead is a headline for its section. Apply the same rules: give details, target something the reader wants, preview what the section delivers.

Rewrite vague subheads as specific ones:
- "Before you start, what are your goals?" → "3 goals: sell extra stock, slow season sales, brand visibility"
- "Connect the dots" → "Here's how to keep all your teams on the same page"
- "Talk about benefits instead of features" → "Benefits beat features... except when features beat benefits"

---

## Format-Specific Notes

**Search posts / SEO content:** The keyword must appear, and searcher intent usually shapes the structure anyway. Use whatever space remains to promise a specific benefit or strip away a potential objection — e.g., "10 Types of Content That Actually Work (Without Wasting Your Time)."

**Customer stories / case studies:** Lead with specific, measurable results. Formula: "How [Customer] got [Result] + [with X / in Y time / by doing Z]." Example: "How a Cooking School Gets 70% Customer Retention with ActiveCampaign."

---

## What a Headline Must Do

Every strong headline accomplishes at least one of these:

1. **Communicate self-interest** — the reader immediately knows what's in it for them
2. **Open a curiosity gap** — the reader needs to read on to close the loop
3. **Deliver news** — something new, different, or surprising
4. **Identify the reader** — "this is for people exactly like you"

The best headlines do two or three at once.

---

## Headline Types and When to Use Them

**Benefit-led** — States the transformation directly. Best for high-awareness audiences, ads, CTAs.
> "Double your email open rates in one week."

**Curiosity-led** — Opens a loop the reader needs to close. Best for content, social, subject lines.
> "Why everything you know about productivity is making you less productive."

**Specificity-led** — The number or detail does the convincing. Best for case studies, reports, list content.
> "17 copywriters share the one change that doubled their conversions."

**Reader-identification** — Names exactly who this is for. Best for segmented audiences, landing pages, targeted ads.
> "For the freelancer who's tired of chasing invoices."

**Belief-surfacing** — Voices an unspoken belief the reader holds. Best when you know your audience's inner monologue.
> "Shouldn't starting a business be easier than this?"

**Story / intrigue** — Creates narrative pull. Best for long-form content, advertorials, email campaigns.
> "They laughed when I sat down at the piano."

## What good looks like

Headlines are the most documented element in copywriting. The examples below span classic direct response, modern SaaS, and conversion-tested before/after pairs. Each is annotated for what the mechanism is and what makes it work.

---

### Specificity and proof through detail

> "At 60 miles an hour the loudest noise in this new Rolls-Royce comes from the electric clock."
> — David Ogilvy

The headline makes no explicit quality claim. It proves quality through a single, hyper-specific detail that the reader has to sit with. The electric clock is the proof. Ogilvy said he spent three weeks finding that detail. The lesson: the specific detail does more convincing than any adjective.

> "A little mistake that cost a farmer $3,000 a year."
> — Victor Schwab

Loss aversion plus specificity. The dollar figure makes the mistake concrete; "little" makes it feel preventable; "a year" compounds the pain. The reader immediately calculates their own version of this mistake. No promise is made — the reader supplies their own motivation.

> "We helped a London-based creative agency generate 143 qualified leads in 12 months."
> — First Round Capital example

The modern equivalent of the Schwab approach: location, number, qualifier ("qualified"), timeframe. Every word is doing work. Compare to the control: "We help you grow your business" — which could be the headline of any agency on earth.

**Template:** `[Number] [specific people] [specific result] in [timeframe]`

---

### Pain-point questions

> "Do you make these mistakes in English?"
> — Max Sackheim (ran continuously for 40+ years)

The word "these" does the critical work — it implies the mistakes are already known and documented, which makes the reader feel the gap in their own knowledge. A yes/no question would fail ("Do you make mistakes in English?"). "These" creates specificity without naming anything, which forces the reader to find out what they are.

> "Are you ever tongue-tied at a party?"
> — Victor Schwab

Names a private shame the reader has never said aloud. The specificity of the situation ("at a party") is what separates it from "do you struggle to talk to people." Anyone who has lived that moment feels seen immediately.

> "Wrestling with projects? It doesn't have to be this hard."
> — Basecamp

The question identifies the pain; the second sentence immediately offers relief without explaining how. The reader's reaction is "yes" then "tell me more." "This hard" implies the reader already knows it's too hard — Basecamp is validating a belief, not introducing a problem.

**Template:** `[Specific embarrassing or costly situation]?` — avoid yes/no; the question should surface something the reader has felt but not said.

---

### Narrative pull

> "They laughed when I sat down at the piano — but when I started to play!"
> — John Caples, 1925

The headline opens mid-scene at the moment of maximum social tension. The reader is already asking: what happened? It works because the structure maps to a universal desire — to prove skeptics wrong — without needing to explain the product at all. Every element earns its place: the laughing creates humiliation, the dash creates suspense, and the incomplete clause forces the reader to complete the story by reading on.

> "Send a potato. It'll still be funny when you're sober."
> — Potato Parcel

Drops the reader into a specific situation (drunk gift-buying) without announcing it. The second sentence is the laugh — and the proof of concept. This is narrative pull compressed to two sentences: situation, then payoff.

---

### Social proof questions

> "Who else wants a screen star figure?"
> — Victor Schwab

"Who else" is the move. It implies others have already gotten this result — the reader is joining a group, not taking a risk. The question creates aspiration without a claim.

> "Which twin has the Toni?"
> — Toni Home Permanent, 1949

Turns product proof into a game. The reader can't know the answer without engaging, and the implication (one twin paid $15 for a salon perm, the other paid $2) does the selling. The headline never mentions price or quality — it just makes the reader look.

**Template:** `Who else wants [desired outcome]?`

---

### Voice of customer

**Control:** "Your addiction ends here."
**Challenger:** "If you think you need rehab, you do."
— Joanna Wiebe for Beachway rehab center, lifted verbatim from an Amazon book review. Result: 400%+ more clicks on the CTA.

The control is confident but brand-centric — it tells the reader what the product does. The challenger is in the reader's voice, addressing the exact internal hesitation they're experiencing. It doesn't sell rehab; it resolves the most important question the reader is sitting with. Wiebe found the line in a review of a book about addiction and used it unchanged.

The principle: when a customer says something in a review, a forum post, or a survey that makes you stop and re-read it — that's a headline.

### Reader identification + objection pre-emption

**Earnable — Ramit Sethi**

> Headline: "Break the shackles of your job, your calendar, and your 9-to-5 income"
>
> Subheadline: "WHAT IF YOU COULD START A BUSINESS THAT PAYS YOU TO LIVE THE LIFE YOU'VE ALWAYS WANTED?"
>
> Even if... you don't have a business idea
> Even if... you're not a world-famous expert
> Even if... you're overwhelmed with no idea where to start

The headline names three specific constraints in sequence — job, calendar, income — which is more activating than a single vague word like "freedom." The subheadline shifts to aspiration. The "Even if..." block is where the real work happens: it names the three objections the reader was forming while reading the headline and removes them before they can take root. Each "even if" addresses a different type of reader who would otherwise have disqualified themselves.

**Find Your Dream Job — Ramit Sethi**

> Headline: "Find Your Dream Job"
>
> Subheadline: "A JOB THAT PAYS YOU MORE, LETS YOU WORK WHERE YOU WANT, WHEN YOU WANT, AND MAKES YOU EXCITED TO GO TO WORK AGAIN"
>
> Even if you don't know what your Dream Job is yet (or if it even exists)
> Even if you don't want to spend 60 hours/week at work
> Even if you don't have the "right" experience on paper
> And especially if you don't feel respected at work, your boss doesn't listen when you talk, you feel worried about the future, and worst of all, you feel stuck and helpless to do something about it

The headline is deceptively plain — three words, no cleverness. The subheadline expands it into specifics: more pay, location freedom, time freedom, emotional re-engagement. The final "Even if" is the longest because it addresses the reader who is furthest from the solution and most likely to leave: the person who doesn't just lack experience, but feels trapped and invisible. Naming "stuck and helpless" is the line that converts the most resistant reader.

**Template:** `[Headline] / [Even if... objection 1] / [Even if... objection 2] / [And especially if... deepest pain]`

### Before/after — specificity rewrites

**Weak:** "Stop Embarrassment"
**Strong:** "Wear what you want. Just the dab of a towelette." — SweatBlock (CopyHackers rewrite, +108% revenue)

The control names the problem but stays in problem-space — it tells the reader what they're trying to escape. The rewrite lands in the outcome: what life looks like after the product works. "Just the dab of a towelette" does double duty — it names the solution mechanism while making it feel effortless.

**Weak:** "Basecamp solves the critical problems that every growing business deals with."
**Strong:** "Don't pay a penny for Basecamp until it doubles your team's productivity." — CopyHackers rewrite exercise

The original is a company-first claim. The rewrite puts the risk entirely on Basecamp — the reader has nothing to lose by trying. "Doubles" is a specific, bold number; "until" signals a guarantee without using the word.

**Weak:** "The world needs wellness." / "Unlock your zen, one massage at a time."
**Strong:** "I'm a Chicago massage therapist who helps women over 40 feel better than they have in months." — ActiveCampaign example

The weak versions are category-level and interchangeable. The strong version names the city (local credibility), the practitioner (person, not brand), the specific audience (women over 40), and a time-anchored result (better than they have in months — not "feel great," not "feel amazing"). Every word eliminates someone it's not for and pulls in someone it is for.

### Reframe and pattern interrupt

> "Speed reading isn't a skill. It's a Chrome extension."
> — Spritz (Justin Blackman)

Reframes the category entirely in five words. The reader expected a pitch for a skill or course; instead the headline collapses the gap between aspiration and solution. The structure — "[thing everyone assumes is hard]. It's [easy solution]" — works because it challenges the premise.

> "One video is worth a thousand words."
> — Loom

Adapts a known phrase to make the product's value self-evident. The reader completes the logic before finishing the sentence.

> "Like a calendar. Unlike any calendar you've ever seen."
> — Motion

The first clause earns trust through familiarity. The second clause creates curiosity. The tension between them is what pulls the reader in. Neither sentence works without the other.

> "Sorry. We don't believe in wedgies."
> — Pair of Thieves (Justin Blackman)

The apology creates warmth and humor before the reader knows what's being sold. "We don't believe in wedgies" names the product's core promise — comfort — through a word the brand's audience would use, not the word "comfort." The tone signals: this brand is human.

---

### SaaS clarity

> "Financial infrastructure to grow your revenue."
> — Stripe

No metaphor, no cleverness. Every word is load-bearing: "financial infrastructure" names exactly what Stripe is; "to grow your revenue" names exactly why you'd use it. The headline works because the product is complex and the audience (developers, finance teams) values precision over poetry.

> "Make your website better. Instantly."
> — Crazy Egg

The timed headline formula at its cleanest. "Make your website better" is the outcome; "Instantly" collapses the objection that improvement takes time. Two sentences, seven words.

### Formulas (CopyHackers) — fill in the blanks

These are tested structures. The blanks should be filled with the reader's specific language, not generic category terms.

- `Get [desired outcome] without [thing they hate doing]`
- `The only [product/service] that doesn't [thing every competitor does that annoys buyers]`
- `Helping [specific type of person] do [specific thing they want to do]`
- `[Do desirable hard thing] like [respected expert], without [expected sacrifice]`
- `Now you can [desired outcome] without [undesirable prerequisite]`
- `Don't pay a penny for [product] until [specific result]`
- `[Number] [people like reader] [result]. You could be next.`
- `Who else wants [outcome others have already gotten]?`
- `[Familiar thing]. Unlike any [familiar thing] you've ever seen.`
- `It's a shame for you not to [desirable outcome] when [evidence others do it easily]`
