# Email

## Subject Line

The subject line's only job is to get the email opened. It should feel like a message from a friend, not a marketing announcement.

Techniques:
- **Curiosity loop:** Give enough to intrigue, not enough to satisfy. The email body closes the loop.
- **Self-centered language:** Address the reader's self-interest directly. "Are you charging enough for your services?" works because it's about them, not you.
- **Conversational tone:** Lowercase, 4–5 words, minimal punctuation. "Lol, thanks for the advice" outperforms "Our Q4 Newsletter — Updates Inside."
- **Contrast and specificity:** Tangible, unexpected, or counterintuitive subjects earn opens.

Avoid: promotional language, exclamation points, spam-trigger words ("free," "guarantee," "limited time"), and anything that sounds like it was written by a company rather than a person.

**Preview text** — treat it as a second subject line. Extend the curiosity; don't repeat the subject line or summarize the email.

---

## Opening Line

The first sentence determines whether the skimmer reads on. It functions as the first step of the slippery slide — its only job is to pull the reader into the second sentence.

Strong openings:
- Drop into a story mid-action: "Last Thursday I was staring at a spreadsheet trying to figure out why the launch flopped."
- Name a specific situation the reader is in: "If you've ever spent three hours on a proposal and heard nothing back..."
- State something surprising or counterintuitive: "Most people who struggle to close are actually working too hard."

Avoid: "Hi [first name], I hope this email finds you well." Signals corporate auto-send and kills engagement immediately.

---

## Body

Every word should be focused on the reader — their situation, their problem, their desired outcome. Not your company, your features, or your process.

Structure:
- **Hook** (established in the opening)
- **Story or substance** — a personal anecdote, case study, insight, or framework
- **Pivot** — where the story connects to the reader's situation: "here's why this matters for you"
- **Takeaway** — what to do with this information
- **Transition to CTA** — a natural bridge, not a bolted-on sell

Bar stool test (Sethi): would you say these exact words to a close friend over drinks? If not, rewrite it. The email should feel like a genuinely useful message from a smart person, not a broadcast.

---

## CTA

One CTA per email. Multiple asks guarantee lower performance on all of them.

The CTA should remind the reader what clicking gets them closer to — not just label the action. Frame it as the natural next step for someone who engaged with what you just shared.

- Good: "If you want to see exactly how I did this — including the template — I broke it all down here: [link]"
- Bad: "CLICK HERE NOW BEFORE IT'S TOO LATE"

Plain text links often outperform buttons in personal email formats because they feel less promotional.

## What good looks like

The examples below are real emails from real brands and creators. Each is annotated with what makes it work and what a stronger version would change.

---

### 1. SaaS welcome email — Typeform

**Subject:** Welcome to Typeform!

> Welcome to Typeform
>
> Happy to have you on board!
>
> Building a typeform is easy. You can start from scratch or get a head start by customizing one of our ready-to-go templates:
>
> Check out more templates
>
> Thanks for using typeforms and making the web more human :)
>
> Happy typeforming!
> The Typeform Team
>
> P.s. Excited as we are? Tweet about it

**What works:** Warm, brief, gets out of the way fast. "Making the web more human" reinforces brand positioning without needing a paragraph to do it. The P.S. attempts virality without being pushy.

**What to improve:** The subject line is the default — nearly every SaaS welcome email uses it. "Happy to have you on board!" tells the reader nothing they couldn't predict. There's no acknowledgment of what the user signed up to accomplish, no pointed first action toward the aha moment, and no indication of what comes next. A stronger version opens with the reader's likely goal, delivers one specific action toward it, and sets expectations for future emails — turning a generic greeting into the first step of onboarding.

### 2. SaaS feature announcement — Figma

**Subject:** Say hello to Figma Plugins

> Special Announcement
>
> Say Hello to Figma Plugins
>
> We are excited to finally introduce you to the first Figma Plugins, brought to you by our amazing beta customers. Explore over 40 (and counting!) plugins that automate work, bring in real data, or flag errors. Or build your own plugin tailored to your design workflow.
>
> Cheers, Team Figma
>
> **Automate repetitive work and flag errors**
>
> Similayer helps you select all layers with the same properties, making it easy to make batch edits. Google Sheets Sync imports content from Google Sheets directly into your Figma file. Color Blind lets designers see their design through the lens of the 8 types of color vision deficiencies.
>
> **Bring real content and data into your design**
>
> Unsplash images can now be inserted straight into your Figma designs. Content Reel populates your designs with relevant text, icons, and images. Mapsicle helps you mock up interactive maps and edit them in-line.
>
> **Extend what's possible in Figma**
>
> Time Machine helps you save and organize work that you want to look back on. Autoflow makes it easy to draw flows between objects and diagram out the UX. Translate helps you test your UI against six different languages to see what may break.
>
> **Be a part of our launch today!**
>
> Tune into our livestream to watch the product announcement live. Join us on Product Hunt as we answer your plugin questions all day. Follow #figmaplugin on Twitter to see the latest plugins released by our community.

**What works:** Features are grouped by benefit category — Automate, Bring real content, Extend — which orients the reader around what they gain rather than what exists. Each plugin description is a one-sentence benefit, not a spec. "Brought to you by our amazing beta customers" is social proof tucked into the announcement rather than a separate section. The closing CTA offers three levels of engagement for three types of readers.

**What to improve:** "We are excited to finally introduce you to..." is a classic weak opener — it centers the brand's excitement, not the reader's payoff. The subject line "Say hello to Figma Plugins" is friendly but doesn't communicate benefit; it would work better as a secondary line under something like "Your design workflow just changed." A stronger email would open with the single most powerful plugin or the most common pain point now solved, then use that to frame everything that follows. The closing CTA section ("Be a part of our launch") drives community participation but doesn't direct users to actually install and use a plugin today.

### 3. SaaS product update / changelog — Loom

**Subject:** [Loom Update] We have new features for you 😉🥓

> You can now share your feedback to a Loom video with another video directly in the comment section.
>
> Easily grant and revoke access to specific people to be able to watch your video. In addition, it is now possible for Google to index Loom videos and drive organic traffic to your content.
>
> You can now easily duplicate your video with a click of a button so you can send more personalized videos and see when they get viewed.
>
> Our new DND feature is available on our Mac app to automatically silence any incoming alerts, emails, and notifications while recording.
>
> We're also delighted to announce our beautiful new Public Roadmap, where we're keeping track of upcoming releases like:
>
> ⚡️ GIF Thumbnails (real soon!)
> 🔊 Internal Audio
> ✍️ Transcription & Captioning
> 📱 Loom for iOS

**What works:** Plain text format creates a low-pressure product-to-user feel. Every item opens with "You can now" — keeping the frame on what the user gains. The roadmap preview creates forward momentum and gives subscribers a reason to stay engaged with future updates.

**What to improve:** The subject line is functional but forgettable — the bacon emoji signals playfulness that the body doesn't deliver on. Each feature gets equal weight as a single sentence, which means none of them get real attention. A stronger update email leads with the subject line naming the single most valuable new capability, opens the body with the problem that capability solves, and then lists the rest below. All five features being presented as equally important means the reader absorbs none of them.

### 4. Ecommerce product story — Tracksmith

**Subject:** How We Remastered the Sweatsuit

> The sweatshirt has been a staple of American sportswear since it was introduced in the early 20th century. When we decided to make our own, the challenge was to do more than just mimic the originals. From materials to trim, we poured over the details that take these sweats from utilitarian to best-in-class.
>
> **A Tale of Cotton**
>
> Armed with a treasure trove of vintage cotton sweatshirts we acquired over the years, we searched for a reverse-weave cotton to stand the test of time. Made from a sturdy blend of 80% cotton and 20% polyester with a soft brushed back, our Trackhouse Sweats get softer and more comfortable with every wash.
>
> **Refined Reinforcements**
>
> Nothing is worse than a sweatshirt that bags out after a few wears. Our ribbed details help the fabric keep its shape and ensure a more refined fit.
>
> **Varsity Details**
>
> The small things make all the difference. From a hidden pocket in our kangaroo pouch to classic elasticated hems on the joggers to a V-stitch finishing on the crew, these finer details elevate the collection beyond the everyday.
>
> In these uncertain times, we're heartened by the fact that running remains a much-needed respite from the challenges of social distancing. So it's only with a great deal of empathy and consideration that we will move forward doing what we do best — sharing our love for running and helping others find deeper meaning in the sport we cherish.

**What works:** The subject line treats the product as a story worth investigating, not a sale. Every paragraph is anchored in a specific material detail — the 80/20 blend, the reverse-weave, the V-stitch — which makes vague quality claims ("best-in-class") actually land because they arrive after evidence. The structure mirrors how a craftsman would explain their work: problem → solution → specific detail, repeated across each section. The reader finishes knowing more about this product than they expected to.

**What to improve:** The opening historical context is unnecessary setup — the email would land harder starting at "When we decided to make our own..." The closing paragraph loses the thread entirely, shifting from product craft to a COVID-era empathy statement. This is where a single clean CTA should land. "Poured over" should be "pored over" — a typo that erodes the credibility a craftsmanship brand is actively building. The email sells through story but forgets to sell: there's no price, no urgency, and the reader has to already know to click.

### 5. Ecommerce abandoned cart — Italic

**Subject:** Still thinking about it?

> You were about to experience everything Italic has to offer...but no worries — we saved your cart for you. Plus, get a **$30 credit towards your purchase** when you become an Italic Bold member today.
>
> [Finish checking out]
>
> Hancock Round Acetate Sunglasses — $30.00 | Qty: 1
>
> [Return to your cart]
>
> Don't see what you're looking for? All you have to do is ask — we'll do our best to track down your request.

**What works:** The subject line avoids the presumptuous "You left something behind!" framing and puts the question back to the reader without accusation. "We saved your cart for you" removes friction — no searching needed. The $30 credit upgrades a basic reminder into an active reason to come back now. Two CTAs — "Finish checking out" and "Return to your cart" — address both the decided and the still-browsing reader.

**What to improve:** "You were about to experience everything Italic has to offer" is brand-centric — it's about Italic, not about the sunglasses the reader actually clicked on. A reader who wants those glasses would respond better to copy that names the specific product and why it's worth returning for. The Bold membership offer introduces a new decision the reader didn't come to make, adding friction at exactly the moment when the goal is to remove it. A stronger version would lead with the specific item left behind, add one sentence about what makes it worth buying, and frame the credit as a closing incentive rather than the lead.

### 6. Story/nurture newsletter — Kat Boogaard

**Subject:** Let's talk about cold emails 😬

> Well hi there,
>
> How was your week? Mine was pretty busy. So busy, in fact, that I have all sorts of exciting things to catch you up on. I'm going to do so in what I believe to be the most efficient way possible — bullet points!
>
> [brief housekeeping: speaking event, conference, freelance workshop]
>
> See? I told you I had a lot to share. Now that we have that (exciting!) housekeeping out of the way, let's get into the meat and potatoes.
>
> This week, I sent a cold email to a prospective client for the first time in a long time. I tweeted about it and, as I expected, so many people responded with their hatred for sending cold emails.
>
> I get it. Like I said, cold emails sort of make me want to throw up too.
>
> But here's the thing: They can also be super effective. That one I sent this week? I received a positive response less than 12 hours later.
>
> **Don't play the numbers game.** You're much better off sending one thoughtful email than blanketing the entire world with a message that's obviously copy-pasted.
>
> **Find personal contact information.** If you submit a request through a website's contact form...it's way tougher to get a response. [describes finding a real person's email]
>
> **Identify a way to connect.** Find some tidbit you can connect over beyond the fact that you want to work with them.

**What works:** The transition from housekeeping to content is warm and explicit — "let's get into the meat and potatoes" — which works because the housekeeping is brief enough to earn it. The core story (cold email → positive response in 12 hours) is an immediate proof point that makes the reader want the lesson. "Cold emails sort of make me want to throw up too" names the reader's resistance before they can object — a strong technique. The writing has a genuine one-on-one voice throughout.

**What to improve:** The housekeeping section runs three bullets before delivering any value. This is asking the reader to wait, and many won't. It should either be cut entirely or moved to a P.S. The subject line generates a click, but the email takes too long to get to the promised content. The three tips are described but not demonstrated — the most valuable thing in this email would be a snippet of the actual cold email that got a reply in 12 hours. That one addition would make the whole piece more credible and more useful.

### 7. Re-engagement — Nomad Copy Agency (Sam)

**Subject:** [not provided — subject line is absent from this example, which is itself a gap]

> Hey, it's Sam.
>
> A few months ago I stepped back from email. I was taking the time to feel out the new direction for my business, and am so excited to send more copywriting and sales resources here in the future.
>
> While I've stepped back from emailing, I've still been out & about in the online space AND in real life! Here's a picture of me at the super cool conference back in December: [photo]
>
> Also, people have been telling me how much they love writing their opt-in pages in a matter of minutes — they're getting wildly good conversions. [Grab the Opt-In Copy Bot Here]
>
> Going forward, you'll hear from me each Monday with practical copy advice and occasional introductions to people I love working with.
>
> If that sounds good, stay. If not, you can unsubscribe here. Either way, I'm glad you're here.

**What works:** The email names the absence directly — it doesn't pretend things have been normal. "I stepped back from email" is honest in a way that builds trust with a reader who's been inactive or ignored. The explicit "stay or go" framing genuinely increases retention by respecting the reader's autonomy. The concrete promise of a Monday cadence resets expectations for what the relationship looks like going forward.

**What to improve:** A re-engagement email lives or dies on the subject line — it needs to earn an open from someone who has been ignoring you, and there isn't one here. The middle section (conference photo + Opt-In Copy Bot plug) dilutes the moment. When someone is deciding whether to remain subscribed, the email should be entirely about them and what they'll gain going forward — not about what you've been up to. The promotional link especially creates distrust: it signals that the email is partly a sales pitch dressed as a return. A stronger version cuts the self-promotion entirely and focuses the whole body on a concrete, specific promise of value for the reader who stays.
