# Search Ad Copy (Google, Bing)

Search ads are intent-based. The viewer is actively looking for a solution — the job is not to interrupt but to confirm they've found what they're looking for. Copy that matches the searcher's language signals relevance before they've read a word.

---

## Technical Structure (Responsive Search Ads)

Google assembles your headlines and descriptions into combinations automatically. This has two implications: every headline must make sense in any position (first, second, or third), and every description must add new information rather than repeat what the headlines say.

- **Headlines:** Up to 15, 30 characters each. Provide at least 10–15 to give Google's algorithm enough combinations to test. Each must stand alone.
- **Descriptions:** Up to 4, 90 characters each. Use all four. Don't waste them repeating headline content.
- **Display URL paths:** Use the path fields to reinforce what the landing page delivers.

---

## Headlines

Include the primary keyword in at least two headlines — Google bolds search terms that match, making the ad stand out visually. Put the most important content (primary keyword, key benefit, or offer) in headline 1 or 2, as these display on all devices.

Each headline must earn its place independently. Since any headline can appear in any position, a headline that only makes sense in context of another will fail when assembled alone.

Use close to the full 30 characters. Longer headlines provide more context and match more query variations.

---

## Descriptions

Descriptions expand on what the headlines established. Each description should accomplish at least two of:
- State a clear benefit in the reader's terms
- Remove a common objection
- Include a specific CTA that tells the reader exactly what happens when they click
- Provide social proof with a number or specific result

Use close to the full 90 characters. Don't repeat headline content — the description is the only space to add new information.

---

## Intent Matching

Match the language of the search query. A searcher scans results for confirmation that this result answers their specific question before reading further. The closer your copy mirrors the terms they searched, the more relevant the ad feels — and the higher your Quality Score.

Specific outcomes outperform vague promises in search. The character constraints mean every word must justify its place — lead with the outcome, not the mechanism.

---

## What good looks like

Search ads are documented by marketers who screenshot and analyze high-performing campaigns. The examples below are drawn from real running ads. Each is annotated with what makes the copy work and what a stronger version would change.

---

### Headline quality — weak vs. strong

**Generic vs. specific outcome**

Weak:
> "Quality HVAC Service"

Strong:
> "Don't Hire Those Guys, Really — We Can Beat Their Prices"
> — HVAC local search ad

The weak version is noise. Every HVAC ad says some version of "quality service." The strong version opens with a direct address to the thing the searcher is actually doing (comparing prices, checking other providers) and then makes a specific claim. "Don't Hire Those Guys, Really" is conversational and confident in a way that stands out from corporate-formatted ads. The qualifier "Really" signals that this is a human speaking, not a template.

---

**Vague promise vs. pre-empted objection**

Weak:
> "Small Business Loans — Apply Today"

Strong:
> "Small Business Loans — Requires $100K+ Annual Revenue"
> — BlueVine

Including a qualification in the headline removes unqualified clicks and increases the quality of those who do click. A business owner who meets that threshold reads this and thinks "that's me." They've pre-qualified themselves. The ad converts more efficiently because the description doesn't have to do screening work.

---

**Category headline vs. niche ownership**

Weak:
> "Buy Swing Sets Online"

Strong:
> "We Do Swing Sets, Nothing Else"
> — King Swing Sets

The weak version competes on the category. The strong version claims a position (specialization) that the searcher can't find elsewhere. "Nothing Else" is the phrase that does the work: it signals focus, expertise, and no upsell, which reduces purchase anxiety for a high-consideration item.

---

**Feature-first vs. objection-first description**

Weak:
> "Top-rated copywriting service. High-quality content delivered fast."

Strong:
> "No Blogspam, No Keyword Fluff. Great Content Isn't a Luxury, It's a Necessity. Request Content From the Top 1% of Writers."
> — ContentFly

The weak description leads with features the reader expects from any copywriting service. The strong version leads with two specific frustrations the searcher already has about the category ("blogspam," "keyword fluff"), which signals understanding before making a claim. "Great Content Isn't a Luxury, It's a Necessity" is a reframe that shifts the decision from "should I buy content?" to "can I afford not to?"

---

### Complete ad example — professional services search

**JustAnswer (query: "legal question lawyer")**

> Headline 1: "Ask a Lawyer: Fraud"
> Headline 2: "Lawyer Will Answer in Minutes"
> Headline 3: "Verified Attorneys Available 24/7"
>
> Description: "Get Answers to Your Fraud Questions From a Verified Lawyer. No Appointment Needed."

**What works:**
Headline 1 mirrors the search query exactly: a searcher who typed "fraud lawyer question" sees their words reflected back, which registers as relevance before they've read anything. Headline 2 answers the searcher's most urgent concern (speed; they need an answer now, not next week). Headline 3 pre-empts the availability objection. The description collapses two remaining objections: the searcher didn't know answers could arrive without a formal appointment, and they may have worried about lawyer credentials. "No Appointment Needed" removes friction; "Verified Lawyer" maintains credibility. Each element addresses a different barrier in sequence.

**What to improve:**
The description doesn't include any price signal or conversion hook. "Get Answers to Your Fraud Questions" tells the searcher what they already know they want. A stronger description would add one specific proof element ("10 million questions answered") or a cost anchor ("From $39") so the searcher can complete a mental calculation before clicking. "From a Verified Lawyer" is weak proof; "verified" is unspecific. A credential or a number (e.g. "12,000+ attorneys") would land harder.

---

### What the character constraint actually forces

Every weak vs. strong pair above illustrates the same underlying principle: the character limit forces a choice between what you want to say and what the searcher needs to hear. Generic headlines ("Quality Service," "Apply Today") survive the constraint because they require no specificity. But they waste it. Every character not used to address a searcher's specific situation, objection, or language is a character working against the click.

The best search ad copy treats the 30-character headline limit not as a restriction but as a forcing function: what is the single most important thing for this searcher to see? Answer that, and the headline writes itself.
