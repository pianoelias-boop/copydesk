# Sales Page & Landing Page

The reader arrived intentionally — they clicked something. They're likely comparing you to alternatives, they can scroll at their own pace, and the stakes are higher than an email click. Everything below is specific to this medium.

For the structural framework organizing the argument (PAS, BAB, AIDA), see `references/frameworks.md`.

---

## Match the Page to Traffic Temperature and Awareness Stage

The top of the page must do two things simultaneously: match the message of the source the reader came from (the ad, email, or social post that brought them here), and match their awareness stage. A mismatch between the source and the page is one of the most common conversion killers — the reader clicked something that promised one thing and landed somewhere that feels different.

**Traffic temperature:**
- **Cold traffic** (ads, unfamiliar audience): Start with the problem and the reader's situation. Earn trust before the offer appears. More lead-in required.
- **Warm traffic** (email list, retargeting, referrals): Can move faster. The relationship was built elsewhere; the page can lean toward the offer sooner.
- **Hot traffic** (previous customers, strong referrals): Lead with the offer, proof, and a clear path to yes. Don't over-explain.

**Awareness stage:** The top 10% of the page should match where the reader is; the bottom 90% moves them toward a decision. A solution-aware reader who lands on a page that re-explains the problem feels talked down to. An unaware reader who lands on a product pitch feels ambushed.

---

## Length Calibration

Length should match three things: price, complexity, and skepticism.

Higher price → more copy needed to justify the investment. More skeptical audience → more proof, more objection handling. More complex product → more explanation of what it is and how it works.

The rule isn't "long copy wins" or "short copy wins." Write exactly as much as it takes to answer every objection and build complete confidence, then stop.

## The Hero Section (Above the Fold)

The hero section is the most valuable real estate on the page. On mobile — now most traffic — it's a small vertical strip that has to do a lot of work fast.

It must communicate three things before the first scroll:
1. Who this is for
2. What transformation it delivers
3. Why this is worth reading further

Clarity beats cleverness. A reader who doesn't immediately understand what the page is about will leave. The subheadline adds texture; the headline's only job is to orient the right reader and pull them in.

Lead with credibility markers early: trust signals, logos, user counts, or a high-impact testimonial near the top signal that this is worth considering before the reader has invested any reading time.

**Example — Earnable (Ramit Sethi):**

> "Break the shackles of your job, your calendar, and your 9-to-5 income"
>
> Subheadline: "WHAT IF YOU COULD START A BUSINESS THAT PAYS YOU TO LIVE THE LIFE YOU'VE ALWAYS WANTED?"
>
> "Introducing Earnable, a totally new way to start and grow your business
>
> *Even if…* you don't have a business idea
> *Even if…* you're not a world-famous expert
> *Even if…* you're overwhelmed with no idea where to start"

The headline is kinetic — "break the shackles" — and names three specific constraints in sequence rather than one vague abstraction. The subheadline shifts from problem to aspiration. The "Even if..." section immediately neutralizes the three most common objections before the reader has time to form them. Compare this to a generic alternative: "Learn How to Start an Online Business" — which is accurate but gives no reason to care and addresses no one's specific situation.

**Classic example — John Caples (1925):**

> "They Laughed When I Sat Down At The Piano — But When I Started to Play!"

This headline opens a story mid-scene, at a moment of maximum social tension. The reader is forced to ask: what happened next? It's been called arguably the most successful mail-order headline of the 20th century. The structure — humiliation reversed — maps directly to the reader's desire to prove skeptics wrong. The mechanism (a correspondence course) doesn't appear in the headline at all.

## The Pain Story

The pain section must be specific and emotionally resonant. Don't describe the problem in general terms — describe the specific moment the reader experiences it. Not "when you're stressed about cash flow" but "at midnight, staring at next month's numbers, running the same calculation again hoping it comes out different."

For solution-aware audiences — readers who have already tried other approaches — name what failed and why. Position your solution against their previous attempts, not against doing nothing. This shows you understand where they actually are.

**Example — Earnable (Ramit Sethi):**

> "Did you dream of waking up every morning to a screaming alarm, hitting the snooze button, scrolling Instagram to see a few beach photos ('That would be nice…someday…'), then stumbling out of bed, gulping down some coffee, and rushing out the door?
>
> When did we *accept* a life that looks like this? Tired, underappreciated, resigned to the same old schedule…because 'that's just how it is.'
>
> And that's life: A 9-5 job, Netflix at night, weekend trips to Home Depot to buy some new shelves, and endless scrolling on Instagram to see photos of places we'll never visit. **For the rest of your life.**"

Every detail is a brand-name stand-in for a specific kind of resignation: Home Depot, Netflix, Instagram. The question "When did we *accept* a life that looks like this?" shifts the pain from circumstance to complicity — which is more activating than sympathy. "For the rest of your life" arrives like a verdict. The reader who recognizes themselves in this description feels precisely understood, not generically pitied.

Immediately after the pain, Earnable pivots to the vision — a specific Monday morning at 9:15am, coffee, no alarm, 13 orders while sleeping. This is the contrast structure: paint the current reality until it's uncomfortable, then paint the desired reality until it feels achievable.

## Visual Rhythm and Readability

Sales pages are scanned before they're read. Readers look for reasons to engage before committing to the full text.

- Short paragraphs — 2 to 4 lines before a visual break
- Subheads every few paragraphs so scanners can follow the argument without reading every word
- Bold text and pull quotes highlight the most important claims — not decoration
- Bullet points earn their place for specifics (what's included, who it's for) but don't replace paragraphs of argument
- White space is part of the design — dense text walls signal effort before the reader has started

## Proof Placement Strategy

Where proof appears matters as much as what it says.

- **Early:** Credibility signals — logos, numbers, "joined by X people" — validate that this is worth considering before the reader has committed to reading
- **Mid-page:** Transformation testimonials and before/after stories, placed right after you introduce the product while the reader is deciding whether to believe your claims
- **Near the final CTA:** Your single most powerful testimonial, plus risk-reduction proof — the guarantee, satisfaction data — to address the last objection standing between the reader and the button

Specificity is everything in testimonials. "This changed my life" is noise. "I closed 3 clients in the first two weeks using exactly what's in module 2" is proof.

**Weak vs. strong — real examples:**

Weak (emotional but unspecific):
> "I can say with absolute certainty that I wouldn't be in the place I am without the coaching program. I have learned so much about what my body is capable of." — Nerd Fitness coaching testimonial

Strong (transformation with numbers):
> "I'm doing over $10,000 per month when previously I thought in a good *year* I might like to do $10,000. Overall, I feel less stress about finances. I used to wake up in the morning and kind of feel like I had to go, go, go." — Bryce Conway, Earnable student

> "This month, we'll end on $30,000 in revenue — our largest month this year — with just under 8,000 subscribers. Because of my online business, my family and I are leaving soon to travel to Europe for 2 months." — Scott Barlow, Earnable student

The first testimonial conveys positive feeling but gives the skeptical reader nothing to evaluate. The strong examples anchor the claim with a specific number, name a before state, and add a detail about what that outcome unlocked — without sounding like a press release.

## Who This Is For / Who This Is Not For

Explicitly excluding some people builds trust with the right ones. "This is not for you if [X]" signals honesty and selectivity. The reader who is the right fit feels more confident they've found something real rather than something marketed at everyone.

**Example — Earnable (Ramit Sethi):**

> "Earnable is NOT for you if:
>
> You're looking for a magic bullet without putting in the challenging work of idea generation, validation, customer research, marketing, pricing, packaging, and positioning.
>
> You're starting from $0 and expect to make $1,250,000 in 8 weeks. Get-rich-quick schemers, please leave.
>
> You have credit card debt. Sorry, your first duty is to pay it off. You will not be allowed in until you do, and if I find out you joined with CC debt, I will ban you from ever purchasing any of my products again."

The last exclusion is deliberately harsh. A softer version would lose the effect — the sharpness signals that the seller actually means it, which paradoxically increases desire among the right readers. Someone who reads "I will ban you" and thinks "he's serious" feels more trust, not less.

## CTA Placement

Don't wait until the end for the first CTA. Place one at each natural decision point — after the product introduction, after the proof section, and at the end. Readers who are convinced early shouldn't have to scroll to act.

Button copy should mirror the transformation, not label the transaction. "Get instant access" beats "Buy now." "Start building my list today" beats "Sign up."

---

## Price Presentation

Reveal the price after the value is established — not before. By the time the reader sees the number, they should have a clear sense of what they're getting and why it matters to them.

Reframe the price relative to something meaningful: the cost of the problem it solves, the value of the outcome it delivers, or the cost of alternatives that haven't worked. The goal isn't to make the price seem cheap — it's to put it in a context where it makes sense.

---

## Guarantee

A strong guarantee removes the last barrier between interest and yes. Place it near the final CTA where purchase anxiety is highest. Make it specific — what's covered, for how long, what the process is — and state it plainly. Hedging or burying the guarantee defeats the purpose.

The strength of a guarantee is proportional to how specifically it names what the buyer gets if things go wrong — and how confident it makes the seller sound. Generic vs. specific vs. bold:

**Generic:**
> "If for any reason you're not satisfied, return the product for a FULL refund."

**Specific:**
> "Your satisfaction is 100% guaranteed. If a course is ever less than completely satisfying, you may exchange it for another of equal value at any time. You may also return any course, within one year of initial purchase, for a full refund." — The Great Courses

**Bold (outcome-guarantee):**
> "If at any time in the first 60 days you feel that we did not live up to our promises and you choose to leave us, we'll pay you back DOUBLE what you've paid us on that package, up to the point that you cancel — no strings and no questions!" — Afrihost

> "If you become a victim of identity theft, LifeLock will spend up to $1 million to hire experts to help your recovery." — LifeLock

> "Our Barista Promise: Love your beverage or let us know. We'll always make it right." — Starbucks

The Starbucks version is the most extreme: no time limit, no conditions, no process to follow, just a human promise. Shorter and more absolute than any policy language, which is exactly why it lands. The Afrihost version inverts the risk entirely — the buyer can only lose by not trying. Both represent the same principle: the guarantee should be as confident as the product claim.

## The P.S.

A significant number of readers scroll directly to the bottom before reading top to bottom. The P.S. must work as a standalone entry point: restate the core offer, the main benefit, and the CTA in two to three sentences. It's often the second most-read element on the page after the headline.
