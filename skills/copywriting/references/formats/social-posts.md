# Organic Social Posts

Social posts live and die by the first line. Most feeds show 1–2 lines before a "see more" cutoff — that first line is your headline and it must work in isolation.

---

## First Line

Give the audience something to agree or disagree with. Posts that provoke a clear reaction outperform posts that inform neutrally.

Techniques:
- **Pain point:** Name a specific frustration the reader is currently experiencing, framed as a root cause they haven't identified yet
- **Hot take / contrarian:** Take a position that challenges a widely held assumption in the reader's field — most effective when you intend to complicate or reverse it in the body
- **Curiosity gap:** Reveal just enough of a surprising outcome or result to make the reader need to know how it happened
- **List promise:** State a specific number of insights, mistakes, or lessons the post will deliver — specificity in the number increases credibility
- **Story entry:** Drop into a high-stakes moment mid-scene, before the outcome is known

---

## Body

- Short paragraphs — 1–3 sentences, visual line breaks between them
- Build toward a payoff — don't frontload everything; create forward momentum
- Each paragraph earns the next
- Concrete specifics: numbers, scenarios, before/afters, direct quotes

---

## Closer / CTA

- A question that invites engagement tied directly to the post's topic
- A soft CTA that asks for a share if the content was useful
- A closing statement that distills the core insight into one memorable sentence

---

## Platform Notes

**LinkedIn:** Credibility-building environment. Longer posts work. Break down a business problem and offer a structured solution. First line is critical — LinkedIn buries text after 2–3 lines. Numbers and specific outcomes perform well.

**Instagram:** Caption is secondary to the visual, but the first line names the specific value the post delivers. Keep it tight — 1–3 sentences; anything longer is often a turnoff.

**Twitter/X:** One compressed insight per tweet, or a thread that escalates in value. The first tweet must justify the rest. Tight and concise; no room for preamble.

---

## What good looks like

Social posts are accessible and frequently reproduced for analysis. The examples below cover LinkedIn and Twitter/X — the two platforms where post text is most independently documented. Each includes both what makes it work and what a stronger version would change.

---

### First line quality — weak vs. strong by technique

**Pain point**

Weak:
> "Burnout is a real problem for high performers."

Strong:
> "High-performers are burning out & quitting in their prime."
> — Justin Welsh

The weak version states a general truth. The strong version names a specific, visible action (quitting in their prime) that creates urgency and makes the reader check whether this applies to them. "In their prime" is the phrase that stings — it raises the stakes from discomfort to loss.

---

**Contrarian / hot take**

Weak:
> "Most people think about productivity the wrong way."

Strong:
> "My secret sauce is less ambition."
> — Justin Welsh

The weak version promises a correction but names nothing specific — any productivity post could use this opener. The strong version is a declaration that contradicts a specific cultural assumption (that more ambition = more success), and it arrives in first person, which adds credibility. The reader immediately wonders: what does he mean? What does he do instead?

---

**Curiosity gap**

Weak:
> "Here are some lessons from 5 years of building online."

Strong:
> "I think an interesting way to live is the 'multi-career' life."
> — Justin Welsh, 1,014 comments

The weak version is a menu. The strong version is a position. "I think" signals this is a genuine perspective, not content marketing. "Multi-career life" is an unfamiliar frame that requires explanation — the reader has to stay to understand what it means.

---

**List promise**

Weak:
> "Tips for writing better LinkedIn posts."

Strong:
> "Why you should consider quitting your job in 2023: 10 reasons."
> — Justin Welsh, 760 comments

Specificity in number adds credibility. But the real work is the word "consider" — it signals this isn't a manifesto, it's a fair argument, which makes the reader more likely to trust what follows. The year adds stakes: this is timely, not timeless advice.

### Complete post example — LinkedIn

**Justin Welsh (19,451 reactions, 982 comments)**

> I left the rat race 24 months ago. My secret sauce is less ambition.
>
> I don't:
> - want to change the world.
> - want to build the next unicorn.
> - want to be featured on any lists.
> - want to get the highest valuation.
>
> Instead:
> - I want to spend my time working on things I enjoy with people I enjoy.
> - I want to be able to travel wherever I want to, whenever I want to.
> - I want to spend way more time with my friends and family.
> - I want to stop doing things I don't like doing.
>
> Thinking about what you actually want in life can force different behavior. Take you down a different path. A path towards living more intentionally.
>
> Are you copying someone else's life? Or designing your own?

**What works:**
The first line is a contrarian hook — "less ambition" is a direct challenge to LinkedIn's prevailing culture of grinding and scale. The "I don't / Instead: I do" structure does the work of showing, not telling: you understand his philosophy from the concrete specifics, not from an abstract claim. The closing is a two-option question that invites reflection, not engagement-bait — it implies that many readers are in the "copying" camp without saying it directly. The post is short enough to read in 30 seconds and leaves the reader feeling something.

**What to improve:**
The list items in the "Instead" section are vague — they describe a feeling rather than a situation. "Spend time with friends and family" is what every LinkedIn post about quitting says. The post would be stronger with at least one concrete detail: where did he travel? What project? One specific image would make the aspiration feel real rather than theoretical. The closing question is strong but asked at the end of what is already a well-known Welsh format — he has posted versions of this same post at 12 months, 18 months, 21 months, 24 months, and so on, which means frequent readers feel the diminishing returns.

### Complete post example — Twitter/X thread

**Naval Ravikant, "How to Get Rich (without getting lucky)" (2018 — 21,000+ retweets)**

Hook tweet:
> "How to Get Rich (without getting lucky):"

Body (selected from the 39-tweet thread):
> "Seek wealth, not money or status. Wealth is having assets that earn while you sleep. Money is how we transfer time and wealth. Status is your place in the social hierarchy."
>
> "You're not going to get rich renting out your time. You must own equity — a piece of a business — to gain your financial freedom."
>
> "Give society what it wants but doesn't know how to get. At scale."
>
> "Escape competition through authenticity."
>
> "Specific knowledge is found by pursuing your genuine curiosity and passion rather than whatever is hot right now."
>
> "Building specific knowledge will feel like play to you but will look like work to others."
>
> "Code and media are permissionless leverage. They're the leverage behind the newly rich. You can create software and media that works for you while you sleep."
>
> "Learn to sell. Learn to build. If you can do both, you will be unstoppable."

Final tweet:
> "Apply specific knowledge, with leverage, and eventually you will get what you deserve."

**What works:**
The hook tweet is the rarest kind — it promises a specific transformation ("get rich") with a pattern interrupt ("without getting lucky") that challenges the cynicism most readers bring to the topic. Each tweet is self-contained: any single one could be taken out of context and still make sense. The tweets are written at a density that rewards slow reading — "Escape competition through authenticity" is six words that contain an entire business philosophy. The thread is structured so that even a reader who drops off midway leaves with something useful. It circulated offline for years, turned into blog posts, books, and podcast series — evidence that the ideas outlasted the format.

**What to improve:**
The hook is abstract — "How to Get Rich (without getting lucky)" doesn't tell you *who* this is for. Someone in a specific situation (broke at 30, corporate employee, early-stage founder) would engage more with a hook that named their circumstance. Several of the 39 tweets are too compressed to be actionable without the surrounding context — "Give society what it wants but doesn't know how to get. At scale." is memorable but leaves most readers without a next step. A thread structured for action (with occasional "here's how to do this") would convert more readers into followers and buyers alongside the philosophical density.
