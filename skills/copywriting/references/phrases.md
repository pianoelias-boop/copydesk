# Phrase-Level Toolkit

These are specific word and phrase constructions with documented persuasive mechanisms. Each one does a distinct job. Use them as shortcuts — places where the phrasing itself carries the technique.

Sources: Joanna Wiebe (CopyHackers), Jonah Berger (*Magic Words*), Phil M. Jones (*Exactly What to Say*), Robert Cialdini (*Influence*).

---

## Objection Handling

**"Even if..."**
Pre-empts a specific reader objection inline, without waiting for the reader to raise it. Pair it with the exact hesitation the reader is most likely to have.
> "You'll get results in 30 days — even if you've never written a word of copy."

**"You're probably thinking..."**
Names the objection before the reader has consciously formed it. The reader feels understood rather than sold to. Follow it with the reframe or the answer.
> "You're probably thinking: I've tried tools like this before. Here's what's different."

**"Before you make your mind up..."**
Re-opens a closing door without pressure. Useful at the end of a sales page or email when the reader is about to leave without converting.
> "Before you make your mind up, here's what most people miss on their first read."

**"I'm not sure if it's for you, but..."**
Reduces resistance by signaling no pressure. The phrase lowers the reader's guard because it doesn't push — which paradoxically makes them more receptive to what follows. (Jones)
> "I'm not sure if it's for you, but we just opened a new cohort."

**"Just out of curiosity..."**
Elicits honest responses without triggering defensiveness. Useful for probing hesitations in emails or for surfacing objections you want to address. (Jones)
> "Just out of curiosity — what's been the biggest obstacle so far?"

## Feature-to-Benefit Bridges

**"Which means..."**
Connects a feature to what it actually does for the reader. Every feature claim should be able to complete this sentence. If it can't, the feature hasn't been translated into a benefit. (Wiebe)
> "The dashboard updates in real time, which means you catch problems before your client does."

**"So that..."**
Links an action or feature to its outcome. Slightly more causal than "which means" — implies the feature was designed with the reader's goal in mind.
> "Invoices go out automatically so that you get paid without chasing."

**"So you don't have to..."**
Frames the benefit as friction removed. Particularly effective when the reader has a strong existing pain around the effort the product eliminates.
> "The system handles follow-ups so you don't have to."

**"Get [X] without [Y]"**
The benefit paired with the sacrifice the reader feared they'd have to make. Names what they want and removes the barrier in the same phrase.
> "Get six-pack abs without giving up carbs."

## Future Pacing

**"Imagine..." / "Just imagine..."**
Pulls the reader into a mental picture of the desired outcome before they've bought anything. The reader's brain begins to experience the result, which increases desire. (Jones / CopyHackers)
> "Imagine opening your laptop on Monday morning to three new leads — without running a single ad."

**"Picture this..."**
Same mechanism as "Imagine" — slightly more cinematic. Works well as an opener for a pain or dream-state scenario.
> "Picture this: it's Friday at 3pm and everything on your list is done."

## Social Proof Framing

**"Most people in your situation..."**
Normalizes the decision through others. Reduces the feeling of risk because the reader isn't being asked to do something unusual — they're joining a majority. (Jones)
> "Most people in your situation choose the annual plan once they see the math."

## Curiosity and Credibility

**"Here's why..."**
Signals that an explanation is coming. Earns the next sentence because the reader knows a reason is about to be given. More credible than a bare claim.
> "This works for beginners. Here's why."

**"Here's the thing..."**
Conversational pivot to the key point. Signals that what comes next is the real substance — cuts through preamble.
> "Here's the thing: most people are solving the wrong problem."

**"The good news is..."**
Frames what follows positively, even when delivering a mixed message. Useful before a pivot or after acknowledging a difficulty. (Jones)
> "The good news is you don't need an audience to start."

## Word-Level Swaps

**Nouns over verbs for identity**
Tying an action to a noun (an identity) is more persuasive than asking for the action alone. People act in accordance with who they believe they are. (Berger)
> "Be a subscriber" outperforms "Subscribe."
> "Become a member" outperforms "Sign up."

**"Don't" over "can't"**
"I don't do that" signals internal values and choice. "I can't do that" signals external constraint. In copy, "don't" implies the reader has agency and standards — "can't" implies limitation. (Berger)
> "We don't lock you into a contract" outperforms "We can't lock you in."

**"Could" over "should"**
"Should" implies obligation and creates mild resistance. "Could" opens possibility without pressure, which leads to higher-quality engagement and less defensiveness. (Berger)
> "You could try the free plan first" outperforms "You should try the free plan first."

**Present tense over past tense**
Present tense implies ongoing, permanent truth. Past tense implies a result that may no longer apply. (Berger)
> "This works for first-time founders" outperforms "This worked for first-time founders."

## CTA Phrasing

**First-person button copy**
Button text written in the reader's voice — what they'd say if they were asking for the thing — outperforms third-person action labels. The reader's internal monologue completes the click. (Wiebe)
> "Send me the free guide" outperforms "Download."
> "Start my free trial" outperforms "Get started."

**"What questions do you have?" over "Do you have questions?"**
The first presumes engagement — the reader has questions, and the only open issue is what they are. The second offers an opt-out. Presume forward motion rather than offering a door to exit. (Jones)

## Proof and Persuasion

**"Because..."**
Adding any reason — even a weak or obvious one — increases compliance. Cialdini's research: people respond to the structure of a reason, not just its content. Always give a "because" after a request or claim.
> "Sign up today, because spots close Friday" outperforms "Sign up today."

**"Don't [X] until you [Y]..."**
Creates a stopping pattern. Positions what follows as essential prerequisite knowledge. The reader pauses because they've been told not to proceed without first knowing something.
> "Don't hire a copywriter until you read this."
> "Don't launch your landing page until you've done this one thing."
