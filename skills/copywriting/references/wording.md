# Wording: Do's and Don'ts

Principles tell you what to say. This document tells you how to say it — at the sentence and word level. These are the mechanical habits that separate copy that reads cleanly from copy that loses the reader mid-sentence.

---

## 1. Active Voice

Passive constructions bury the actor and soften the claim. Active voice puts the subject doing the thing, which is cleaner, faster, and harder to ignore.

The test: find the verb and ask who's doing it. If the answer isn't the subject of the sentence, it's passive.

**Passive:** "Your results will be tracked automatically."
**Active:** "You'll see your results update in real time."

**Passive:** "Thousands of businesses have been helped by this approach."
**Active:** "Thousands of businesses use this to cut their reporting time in half."

**Passive:** "Your data is protected by 256-bit encryption."
**Active:** "256-bit encryption protects your data."

The passive voice isn't always wrong. Use it when the actor genuinely doesn't matter or isn't known — "the package was left at the door," "the file was corrupted" — or when you're deliberately softening blame. But in persuasive copy, passives almost always weaken the sentence. They signal distance and corporate caution. Active voice signals a writer who means what they say.

**Gobbledygook:** Watch for long noun chains where the verb has been buried or eliminated entirely. "The facilitation of customer onboarding processes" means "helping customers get started." "Implementation of a solution" means "solving it." Any time a sentence can be cut by more than half by finding the verb, it was hiding one.

---

## 2. Sentence Length Variation

Copy that runs at the same length and rhythm puts readers to sleep. Varying length creates the feeling of natural speech — momentum, emphasis, pause.

A longer sentence earns its place when it needs to build — when the idea has parts that connect, when the reader needs to be carried through a sequence of details before the point lands. A short sentence earns its place when the point is the whole thing.

One word can be a sentence. So can two.

**Flat — same length throughout:**
> "You can track your pipeline in real time. You'll get alerts when deals go cold. You can also share reports with your team. Everything updates automatically."

**Varied:**
> "You can track your pipeline in real time, get alerts when deals go cold, and share reports with your team the moment they update. Everything syncs automatically. No manual entry."

The second version groups related ideas into one longer sentence that builds, then lands two short punches. The rhythm signals confidence. The flat version reads like a feature list read aloud.

Andy Crestodina: no paragraph longer than four lines. Occasionally, a single sentence stands alone for emphasis. Or a single word.

## 3. Subject–Verb–Object Order

The clearest sentences put who does what to whom in sequence: Subject → Verb → Object. Disrupting that order makes the reader backtrack.

**Inverted:**
> "For teams that need real-time visibility into their projects, this tool was built."

**Clear:**
> "This tool gives your team real-time visibility into every project."

The inverted version buries the subject ("this tool") at the end of the sentence. The reader has processed a full clause before knowing what the sentence is about.

Subordinate clauses that open a sentence push the subject back. They're not always wrong — a short, punchy opener clause can work — but when the clause runs longer than the main sentence, the sentence has its priorities backwards.

**Buried subject:**
> "Because of the way invoices are batched and processed at the end of each billing cycle, your payment may be delayed."

**Subject first:**
> "Your payment may be delayed — invoices batch at the end of each billing cycle."

The fix is always the same: find the actor, move it to the front, and let the verb follow immediately.

## 4. You and Your Over We and Our

Copy that leads with "we" centers the brand. Copy that leads with "you" centers the reader. The reader doesn't care about the brand — they care about their own situation.

**Brand-centered:**
> "We built this platform to help teams collaborate more effectively. Our mission is to make work feel less like work."

**Reader-centered:**
> "You spend less time in status meetings and more time on the work that matters."

The brand-centered version is about the company's intentions. The reader-centered version is about the reader's day. Same product, different frame — and only one of them answers the question the reader is actually asking: what's in it for me?

Joanna Wiebe's principle: default to the reader's perspective and address them directly with "you."

**Exceptions:** Founder voice, brand story, and transparency moments ("here's how we handle your data") are places where "we" is appropriate and expected. The rule is about default orientation, not a ban on first person.

## 5. Front-Load Every Sentence and Line

The value proposition belongs at the start of a sentence, not the end. When the mechanism, condition, or context comes first, the reader has to process setup before they get to the reason they should care.

**Buried (real, Tracksmith):** "Made from a sturdy blend of 80% cotton and 20% polyester with a soft brushed back, our Trackhouse Sweats get softer and more comfortable with every wash."

**Front-loaded (rewrite):** "Trackhouse Sweats get softer and more comfortable with every wash, made from 80% cotton and 20% polyester with a soft brushed back."

The value — getting softer and more comfortable with every wash — moves to the front. The material details follow as support. The reader gets the reason to care before the explanation of how.

Joanna Wiebe's two-inch rule: the leftmost content on every line should carry meaningful, benefit-driven information. Opening clauses that exist only to set up the real point are either cut or moved to the middle.

This applies at every level: the first word of a sentence, the first sentence of a paragraph, the first paragraph of a page.

## 6. Open Questions Over Closed Questions

Yes/no questions let the reader answer in one mental syllable and stop. Open questions — what, how, why — pull the reader into picturing their own situation, which keeps them inside the copy.

**Closed:** "Do you struggle to keep your team aligned?"
*(Reader answers yes or no and moves on.)*

**Open:** "What does your Monday morning look like when three projects are running at once?"
*(Reader pictures it. Now they're in the copy.)*

The exception: a closed question can work when the word "these" or "this" is doing the heavy lifting. Max Sackheim's "Do you make these mistakes in English?" is technically a yes/no question, but "these" implies the mistakes are already known and documented — which creates a knowledge gap the reader has to close. The question pulls because of the implied specificity, not because of the question format.

## 7. Use Customer Language Verbatim

The words customers use to describe their own pain are almost always more powerful than the words a writer would choose. When copy uses the reader's own language back at them, it creates instant recognition — they feel understood rather than marketed to.

**Generic (writer's language):** "Inconsistent revenue makes it hard to plan ahead."

**Verbatim (customer's language):** "I still don't know if I can make payroll on the first of every month."

The first version uses category language any writer might reach for. The second uses the specific moment a real business owner has described in their own words. That specificity is what makes the reader stop.

Joanna Wiebe: the best headline she ever wrote — "If you think you need rehab, you do" — was lifted verbatim from an Amazon book review. It outperformed the control by over 400% on click-through.

Where to find customer language: product reviews (Amazon, G2, Trustpilot), support tickets, sales call transcripts, survey open-ends, Reddit threads, forum posts. When a phrase makes you stop and re-read it, that's the one to use.

## 9. Avoid Nominalization

Nominalization is the habit of turning verbs into nouns. It drains energy from sentences and adds words without adding meaning.

| Instead of | Use |
|---|---|
| Make a decision | Decide |
| Give consideration to | Consider |
| Reach an agreement | Agree |
| Conduct an investigation | Investigate |
| Provide assistance | Help |
| Take action | Act |
| Make an improvement | Improve |
| Offer a recommendation | Recommend |
| Have a discussion | Discuss |
| Come to a conclusion | Conclude |
| Make an assumption | Assume |
| Give approval to | Approve |
| Make a suggestion | Suggest |
| Perform an analysis | Analyze |
| Conduct a review | Review |
| Provide support | Support |
| Have an impact on | Impact / affect |
| Make a contribution | Contribute |
| Show a preference for | Prefer |
| Reach a resolution | Resolve |
| Place an order | Order |
| Make a payment | Pay |
| Hold a meeting | Meet |
| Give an explanation | Explain |
| Issue a warning | Warn |

The tell: if a sentence contains "make a ___," "give a ___," "conduct a ___," "have a ___," or "provide a ___," the noun after it is probably a hidden verb. Find the verb and use it directly.

## 10. Cut Filler and Throat-Clearing

Filler phrases consume space without adding meaning. They signal uncertainty and slow the reader before the actual point.

**Common fillers to cut:**

| Instead of | Use |
|---|---|
| In order to | To |
| Due to the fact that | Because |
| At this point in time | Now |
| In the event that | If |
| It is important to note that | (cut entirely — if it's important, just say it) |
| As you may already know | (cut) |
| I wanted to reach out because | (cut — start at the reason) |
| What I mean by that is | (cut — just say the thing) |
| It goes without saying | (cut — if it goes without saying, don't say it) |
| At the end of the day | (cut) |
| With that being said | (cut) |
| To be honest with you | (cut) |

**Throat-clearing openers** are preambles that exist before the actual point of a sentence or paragraph. Andy Crestodina calls these "long, mushy throat-clearing language." The fix is always the same: delete everything before the real point and start there instead.

**Throat-clearing:** "In these uncertain times, as businesses everywhere face new challenges, it's more important than ever to have the right tools."

**Direct:** "The right tools matter more when conditions are unpredictable."

## 11. Contractions

Contractions make copy sound like a person wrote it, and that's exactly the effect you want. "You'll" is warmer than "you will." "Don't" is more direct than "do not." "It's" reads faster than "it is."

**Without contractions:** "You will not be charged until you are ready to upgrade. We do not require a credit card to get started."

**With contractions:** "You won't be charged until you're ready to upgrade. No credit card required to get started."

Use contractions as the default in any copy meant to feel personal: emails, landing pages, social posts, ads. Reserve the formal version for legal disclaimers, terms of service, or contexts where the brand voice is explicitly formal.

The one exception: emphasis. "Do not do this" hits harder than "don't do this" when the stakes are high enough to warrant it.

## 12. Parallel Structure

When items appear in a series or list, they should follow the same grammatical form. Broken parallelism creates friction the reader may not consciously notice but will feel.

**Broken:** "The course covers writing headlines, how to structure a landing page, and email."

**Parallel:** "The course covers writing headlines, structuring landing pages, and writing emails."

**Broken:** "Benefits include faster onboarding, your team will stay aligned, and it reduces churn."

**Parallel:** "Benefits include faster onboarding, better team alignment, and lower churn."

The rule applies everywhere items appear in sequence: bullet lists, "Even if..." blocks, benefit stacks, subheadlines, CTA copy, and any series of three or more items. When one item in a list starts with a verb, they all should. When one starts with a noun, they all should.

## 13. Weasel Words and Hedging Language

Hedges signal uncertainty and invite the reader to doubt alongside you. Words like "might," "could," "potentially," "in some cases," "up to," and "may" reduce the perceived strength of any claim.

**Hedged:** "Customers may see results in as few as 30 days."

**Direct:** "Most customers see results in 30 days."

The hedged version sounds like a legal disclaimer. The direct version sounds like a confident claim — which is more persuasive, even though it's actually more specific and therefore easier to hold the brand accountable to.

Common hedges to cut or replace:

| Hedged | Direct |
|---|---|
| May / might / could | Will / does |
| In some cases | Often / usually / most |
| Up to X | X (if that's the typical result) |
| Potentially | (cut) |
| In many ways | (cut) |
| Sort of / kind of | (cut) |
| Somewhat | (cut) |
| Generally speaking | (cut) |
| It could be argued that | (cut — just make the argument) |

If the direct version of a claim can't be defended, the claim needs tightening, not hedging.

## 14. Use Specifics Instead of Clichés

When a cliché feels right, it's a sign there's a more specific true thing underneath it. Find that and say it instead.

**Common offenders in marketing copy:**

game-changer, move the needle, best-in-class, world-class, cutting-edge, revolutionary, next-level, unlock your potential, seamless, frictionless, robust, synergy, leverage, empower, transform, elevate, disrupt, innovative, holistic, scalable, end-to-end, best-of-breed, mission-critical, value-add, thought leader, circle back, take it to the next level

**The replacement test:** remove the cliché and ask what's left. If the sentence still makes the same claim, the cliché was covering an absence. Find the specific true thing it was gesturing at and say that instead.

**Cliché:** "Streamline your workflow with our revolutionary, best-in-class platform."

**Specific:** "Cut the time your team spends on status updates from two hours a week to fifteen minutes."

The specific version is harder to write because it requires knowing the actual result. That's the point.

## 15. The Humanizer: Writing in Human Voice [DRAFT]

AI-generated copy has recognizable patterns. These patterns appear because language models are trained to be helpful, structured, and thorough — which produces a distinct style that human readers increasingly recognize and discount. If copy sounds like it was generated, it loses the authority and intimacy that makes copy convert.

Patterns to eliminate:

**The em dash pivot ("It's not X — it's Y"):** This construction appears constantly in AI copy because it feels like a reframe. It's become a tell. Replace with a period and a new sentence, or rewrite the claim so it doesn't need the contrast.

**The list of three:** AI defaults to triads — "clarity, confidence, and control," "fast, affordable, and reliable." Real human writers pick one specific thing and say it fully. If three things appear in copy, make sure each one earns its place independently.

**Parenthetical asides:** AI adds parentheticals to hedge, qualify, or show nuance (like this). Human writers either commit to the thought or cut it. Parentheticals mid-sentence slow the reader and signal that the writer wasn't sure whether to include the thought.

**The setup-and-qualify construction:** "While X is important, Y is even more critical." AI uses this to appear balanced. It adds a clause that weakens the main point before making it. Cut the while-clause; lead with the point.

**Hollow affirmations before pivoting:** "Great question." "That's a really interesting point." "Absolutely." These appear in AI responses and in AI-influenced copy as warmth signals. They're noise.

**Generic power words:** "Unlock," "transform," "elevate," "empower," "seamlessly," "robust," "leverage." These feel meaningful but carry no specific claim. Each one should be replaced with the concrete thing it's gesturing at.

**The conclusion that restates:** AI summaries tend to repeat what was just said: "In summary, we've covered X, Y, and Z." Human writers end on something new — a final turn, an implication, a call to act — not a restatement.

**Short, choppy sentences stacked in a row:** AI breaks ideas into the shortest possible units. Human writers vary length — a longer sentence that builds earns the short one that lands. Three short sentences in a row is a signal to consolidate or resequence.

The test: read the copy and ask whether a specific human writer with a specific point of view wrote this. If the answer is "it could have been generated," it probably has too many of these patterns. The fix is almost never adding more content — it's cutting the scaffolding and leaving the real thing.
