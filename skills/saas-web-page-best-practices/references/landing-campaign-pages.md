# Landing & Campaign Pages

Landing and campaign pages are designed for one audience, one offer, one action. They are typically the destination for paid traffic, email campaigns, or specific outbound sequences. Unlike feature pages, they should have no navigation, no exits, and no ambiguity about what the visitor is supposed to do next.

---

## What Makes Landing Pages Different

The key difference from any other page: the visitor arrived with intent. They clicked an ad, a link in an email, or a social post because something promised them value. Your job is to honor that promise and make it easy to claim.

This means:
- The headline must match the message that brought them (message match)
- The page does one thing only
- Every element serves the conversion goal or is cut

---

## Message Match: The Most Important Principle

Message match is the alignment between what the visitor was promised before they clicked and what they see when they land. Poor message match is the most common cause of high bounce rates on pages that are otherwise well-designed.

The headline and hero of the landing page should mirror the language of the source:
- If the ad said "Free 14-day trial for marketing teams" then the headline should echo that promise directly
- If the email said "See how [Company] cut onboarding time by 40%" the page should lead with that result

Do not make visitors recalibrate. They clicked because something resonated. Confirm it immediately.

## Page Structure for Landing Pages

### Remove Navigation

Landing pages should have no nav menu. Every link is an exit. The only path forward is the CTA. The only path away is the browser back button. This is intentional.

Exception: if the page is also serving organic search traffic, a minimal nav may be warranted. But for pure paid or campaign traffic, remove it.

### Hero Section

The hero must do three things in 8 seconds:
1. Confirm this is the right place (message match)
2. Deliver the core value proposition
3. Make the next action obvious

Hero headline: Lead with the outcome or the promise. Not the product name or category.
Sub-headline: Add specificity, mechanism, or audience targeting.
CTA: Above the fold, always. Even if visitors will scroll, the CTA should be visible without scrolling.

Visual: Show the product, the result, or the transformation. Not a stock photo of a handshake or a happy team.

### The Single CTA Rule

One landing page, one CTA. Not "Start free trial" and "Book a demo" and "Learn more" on the same page. Pick the one action that fits where this audience is in their journey.

If you are unsure which CTA to use, choose based on traffic temperature:
- Cold traffic (awareness campaigns): lower-friction CTA like "Get the free guide" or "See how it works"
- Warm traffic (retargeting, nurture emails): higher-commitment CTA like "Start your free trial"
- Hot traffic (branded search, high-intent paid): direct CTA like "Start free" or "Get a demo today"

### The Offer

The offer is everything that goes into what the visitor gets. It is not just the product feature or the free trial. It is the full package of value they receive when they click.

Make the offer explicit. Do not assume visitors will infer what they get. State it directly:
- What they receive
- What it costs (or that it is free)
- What the risk is (none, because no credit card, cancel anytime, etc.)

Visitors who do not understand the offer do not convert.

### Social Proof Placement

On landing pages, social proof has a specific job: reduce the risk of saying yes. Place it:
- Immediately after the hero (logos or a brief stat)
- Adjacent to the CTA (one specific result quote)
- In a dedicated section mid-page (deeper testimonials with context)

The most effective landing page social proof is:
- Specific to the audience the page targets
- Includes a measurable result, not just sentiment
- Comes from someone the visitor can identify with (same role, same company size)

### Form Design

If the page uses a form rather than a direct CTA button:
- Ask for the minimum viable information
- Every additional field reduces conversion
- Name and email converts better than name, email, phone, company, role, and team size
- If you need qualification data, get it after the initial conversion, not before
- Label placement: labels above fields convert better than placeholder-only text on mobile

### The Length Question

Landing pages should be exactly as long as necessary to overcome the visitor's remaining objections and get them to act.

Short pages work for: free offers, low-commitment asks, retargeting audiences who already know the product
Long pages work for: high-price offers, skeptical audiences, cold traffic that needs education

A useful test: read the page draft and note every unanswered question you have as a visitor. If questions remain, the page is too short. If every section is repeating something already covered, it is too long.

## Campaign Page Considerations

Campaign pages tied to a specific promotion, event, or message have an additional constraint: they expire. Design with this in mind.

Urgency on campaign pages:
- Real deadlines convert. Fake countdown timers do not — and actively damage trust when visitors return after the timer resets.
- If the campaign has a genuine end date, use it explicitly: "Offer ends March 31" not "Only 3 days left" on a timer that resets.

Message consistency across campaign assets:
- The ad, the email, the social post, and the landing page should feel like one continuous message, not separate assets that happen to share a URL.
- Use the same headline language, the same visual vocabulary, and the same offer framing across all touchpoints.

Segmented campaign pages:
- If you are running the same campaign to different audiences (e.g., agency vs. in-house, SMB vs. enterprise), build separate pages for each segment even if the offer is identical.
- Segment-specific social proof, language, and pain points convert dramatically better than generic copy.

## Common Landing Page Mistakes

1. Message mismatch. The ad promised one thing and the page says another. The visitor feels tricked and bounces.

2. Navigation left in. Every link is an exit. Remove the nav on dedicated campaign pages.

3. Multiple CTAs. "Start trial" and "Book demo" and "Download guide" all on one page splits attention and kills conversion.

4. Vague offer. The visitor does not understand what they are actually getting when they click. Spell it out.

5. Generic social proof. "Loved by thousands of companies" does nothing. "Used by 3,400 marketing teams at companies like [logos]" builds confidence.

6. Long forms asking for too much upfront. Get the minimum viable information. Qualify later.

7. Not testing. Landing pages are the single highest-leverage asset to A/B test in a SaaS marketing stack. If you are not running tests, you are leaving conversion on the table.
