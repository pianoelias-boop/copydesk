# Feature & Solution Pages

Feature and solution pages are mid-funnel pages that speak to product-aware or solution-aware visitors. They have a specific job: make one capability or use case so clear and compelling that the visitor moves deeper into the funnel.

---

## What Makes Feature and Solution Pages Different

They are not homepages. Visitors arriving on a feature page already know what your product is. They want to know if this specific capability will solve their specific problem. The copy job shifts from "what is this company" to "does this feature solve my exact problem."

They are not landing pages. Unlike campaign pages, feature pages often live in nav, get organic traffic, and serve visitors at multiple awareness stages. They need to work harder across levels.

The conversion goal is usually further engagement: start a trial, book a demo, or navigate deeper. Not an immediate purchase.

---

## Page Structure for Feature Pages

### Hero Section

The hero is not the place for category positioning. Visitors already know the category. Go straight to the specific value this feature or solution delivers.

Hero headline formula: [Outcome the feature delivers] for [specific user]
Example: "Give your sales team pipeline visibility without the manual CRM updates"

Avoid naming the feature in the headline without explaining what it does for the visitor.

Sub-headline: Add the mechanism or the "how." One sentence explaining the key differentiator or how the outcome is achieved.

### Problem Section

Only include a problem section if the problem is genuinely non-obvious. For well-understood pain points, name it fast and move on. Do not spend three paragraphs agitating a problem the visitor already feels — they came to your page, they know they have the problem.

For nuanced or less-obvious problems, use this section to reframe the problem in a way that makes your solution the obvious answer.

### Feature Showcase

Show the product. UI screenshots, short video clips, or interactive demos dramatically outperform copy-only descriptions. The visual should answer "what does it actually look like to use this."

Caption every visual with a benefit statement, not a feature description.
Feature description: "Drag-and-drop workflow builder"
Benefit caption: "Build any automation in minutes without engineering support"

### Benefits Section

3 to 4 benefits maximum. Each benefit should:
- Lead with the outcome, not the feature attribute
- Have a tangible proof point beneath it (a stat, a customer quote, a before/after)
- Be differentiated from what competitors offer on the same feature

Avoid bullet lists of 8 to 12 features. Compress into 3 strong benefits with evidence.

### Social Proof

For feature pages, the most effective social proof is specific to the use case, not generic company endorsements.

Best: "Since using [feature], we reduced manual data entry by 70%" with Role and Company
Weaker: "Amazing platform, highly recommend" with Role and Company

Pull testimonials that mention the specific pain this feature solves or the specific outcome it delivers.

### Objection Handling

Feature pages are where technical objections live. Anticipate and address:
- Integration questions: Does this work with the tools they already use?
- Complexity questions: How long does setup take?
- Trust questions: Is data secure?
- Pricing questions: Is this included in my plan or an add-on?

These can live in an FAQ or be woven into copy near relevant sections. Do not save them for a sales call.

### CTA Strategy

Feature pages typically need two CTA types:
1. High-intent: "Start free trial" or "Get a demo" for visitors ready to act
2. Lower-intent: "See how it works" or "Watch a 3-minute overview" for visitors who need more

Both should be present. Let the visitor choose their readiness level.

## Solution Pages vs. Feature Pages

Solution pages are organized by use case or buyer role rather than by product feature. A solution page answers "do you have a solution for my specific situation" rather than "what does this feature do."

Key differences:
- Solution pages lead with the customer's job-to-be-done, not the product capability
- Solution pages often show multiple features working together toward one outcome
- Solution pages are frequently the entry point for paid search traffic targeting job-aware queries

Structure for solution pages follows the same pattern above, but the hero should lead with the customer's world before introducing how the product solves it.

Example solution page hero for a CRM:
"Your sales team is losing deals in the follow-up. Here is how [Product] makes sure nothing slips."

This is more effective than leading with the product capability because it meets the visitor in their frame, not yours.

---

## Common Feature Page Mistakes

1. Writing for the product team, not the buyer. Feature pages often read like internal documentation. Translate capability into customer outcome at every sentence.

2. Showing too many features. One page, one job. If you have 12 features, each should have its own page, or they should be grouped into 3 to 4 solution areas.

3. No social proof specific to the use case. Generic logos build some credibility but do not answer "has anyone like me used this for exactly this problem."

4. Burying the differentiation. What makes this feature different from a competitor's version should be in the first scroll, not the fifth.

5. Weak or absent CTAs. Feature pages get organic traffic and often rank well. If the CTA is buried or generic, that traffic does not convert.
