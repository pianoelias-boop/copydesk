# SaaS Web Page Best Practices

This skill covers the full craft of high-converting SaaS web pages from upstream messaging strategy down to individual CTA copy. Scope: feature/solution pages and landing/campaign pages.

Organized by discipline:
1. Messaging and Positioning
2. Copywriting
3. Page Structure and Design
4. CTAs and Conversion Mechanics

## Before You Write Anything

Answer these four questions first. If you cannot answer them clearly, the page will underperform no matter how good the copy is.

1. Who specifically is this for? (Role, company type, pain state, not "SMBs" or "marketers")
2. What are they trying to do or avoid? (Jobs-to-be-done, not features)
3. Why would they choose this over the default alternative? (The status quo counts as a competitor)
4. What is the single action this page should drive?

These are upstream positioning questions, not copy questions. Get them right first.

## 1. Messaging and Positioning

### The Five Messaging Layers (Peep Laja / Wynter)

Every SaaS page should score well on all five. Most fail on Differentiation and Friction.

Clarity: Do I understand what this is? Failure mode: jargon, vague abstractions, category-speak.
Relevance: Is this for me and my situation? Failure mode: generic audience, no ICP signal.
Value: Do I want what this promises? Failure mode: feature lists instead of outcomes.
Differentiation: Why this over alternatives? Failure mode: same claims as every competitor.
Friction: What is stopping me? Failure mode: unanswered objections, missing proof.

Run any page draft through all five before finalizing.

### The 8-Second Test (Emily Kramer / MKT1)

The most common failure on any SaaS page: visitors cannot answer "who is this for, what does it do, and why is it better?" within 8 seconds of landing. This applies to feature and campaign pages, not just homepages.

Your hero or opening section must answer all three explicitly, not implicitly.

Weak: "The platform built for modern teams"
Strong: "Email automation for e-commerce brands doing $1M to $50M, without the Klaviyo complexity"

### Positioning Precedes Copy (April Dunford)

If visitors keep asking "but how is this different from X?", the problem is upstream. You have not made your competitive context clear. The five components of effective positioning that should inform every page:

1. Competitive alternatives: what would they use if you did not exist?
2. Unique attributes: what do you do or have that alternatives do not?
3. Value: what does that uniqueness actually deliver?
4. Target customer: who cares most about that value?
5. Market category: what frame makes your value obvious immediately?

Your page should make the answer to number 5 unmistakable within seconds of arrival.

### Specificity as Credibility

Vague claims trigger skepticism. Specific details trigger belief because they imply measurement and imply a real case.

Weak: "Improve your team's efficiency"
Strong: "Cut weekly reporting from 4 hours to 20 minutes"

Replace every summarized pain or benefit with a tangible, specific version. Ask: could a competitor say the exact same thing? If yes, it is not differentiated enough.

## 2. Copywriting

### Write to "You," Not About "We"

SaaS web pages almost universally default to talking about the company: "We built this," "Our platform does," "We help teams." This is the wrong orientation. The visitor does not care about the company — they care about what changes for them.

The default voice on any SaaS page should be second person. Every sentence should be oriented toward the visitor, not the vendor.

Weak (company-focused): "We help marketing teams automate their campaigns."
Strong (visitor-focused): "You send the right message to every contact, automatically."

Weak: "Our platform integrates with over 800 tools."
Strong: "Connect the tools you already use in minutes."

Weak: "We were founded in 2015 to solve the problem of..."
Strong: Cut it. No one reads the founding story in a hero section.

This applies to CTAs too. "Start your free trial" outperforms "Try our product free" because it centers the visitor's action, not the company's offering.

Audit rule: if any section has more instances of "we/our" than "you/your," rewrite it. If you can replace "you" with "we" and the sentence still makes sense, the sentence is wrong.

### The Rule of One (Joanna Wiebe / Copyhackers)

Every page has exactly one reader, one offer, one big idea, and one promise. Commit to one ICP per page. If you serve multiple segments, build separate pages. Trying to speak to everyone means converting no one.

### Stages of Awareness

Match your copy to where the visitor is in their journey. Speaking to someone at the wrong awareness stage is the most common reason well-written copy does not convert.

Problem-aware: Knows the pain, not the solution. Lead with agitating the problem and introducing the category.
Solution-aware: Knows solutions exist, comparing options. Lead with differentiation and proof.
Product-aware: Knows your product, considering it. Handle objections, reduce friction, strong CTA.
Most aware: Ready to act. Get them to the action fast with minimal copy.

Landing and campaign pages typically speak to solution-aware or product-aware visitors. Feature pages typically speak to product-aware visitors. Do not over-explain to people who already get it.

### The PAS Framework

Problem, Agitate, Solution. The most durable structure in conversion copywriting.

Problem: Name the pain in the visitor's exact language, not your internal framing.
Agitate: Show what continuing with the problem costs in time, money, opportunity, or frustration.
Solution: Introduce your product as the bridge to the desired state.

Use voice of customer (VOC) language pulled from customer interviews, reviews, support tickets, and sales calls. When copy sounds like the reader talking to themselves, resistance drops.

### Writing the Value Proposition

A strong SaaS value proposition answers: what is it, who is it for, what does it do for them, and why is it the better choice. It should be a headline plus supporting subhead.

Formula: [Outcome] for [whom] [key differentiator or mechanism]
Example: "Get your data pipeline to production in days, without the custom engineering"

The headline delivers the promise. The subhead adds the mechanism, the for-whom, or the proof. Never be clever at the expense of clear.

### Copy Don'ts

- No "we," "our," or "us" as the default voice — write in second person ("you/your") throughout
- No "robust," "seamless," "powerful," "intuitive," "best-in-class," or "revolutionary"
- No "we help teams do X better" without specifying what better means in measurable terms
- No feature names in headlines, lead with the outcome the feature enables
- No passive voice on CTAs
- Do not claim things every competitor also claims
- Avoid "That's not X, it's Y" constructions, reads formulaic
- Avoid excessive em dashes and formulaic sentence structures
- No stacking adjectives instead of adding specifics

## 3. Page Structure and Design

### Attention Ratio (Unbounce / Oli Gardner)

Every page has an attention ratio: the number of things a visitor could do vs. the number you want them to do. Ideal is 1:1.

Landing and campaign pages: remove nav, reduce all exits, one CTA path.
Feature pages: can have nav, but keep the primary CTA dominant and repeated throughout.

### Above the Fold

Visitors decide within 8 seconds whether to keep reading. The above-fold section must contain:

- A clear headline with your value proposition
- A supporting subhead with specificity or audience targeting
- A primary CTA
- A visual that reinforces not decorates, such as product UI, screenshot, or a result

What it should NOT contain: generic stock photos, vague taglines, animated backgrounds that distract, or multiple competing CTAs.

### Section Structure

Every section should answer a specific visitor question. High-performing section order:

1. Hero: what is it, who is it for, why does it matter (CTA)
2. Social proof bar: logos or "trusted by X" before asking for anything
3. Problem or context: why does this matter now, what is painful about the status quo
4. Solution or how it works: what does the product actually do (show, do not tell, UI visuals work)
5. Benefits plus proof points: 2 to 3 key benefits, each with a tangible proof point beneath
6. Social proof depth: testimonials with specific results, case study snippets
7. Objection handling: top 2 to 3 reasons someone would not convert, addressed directly
8. Final CTA: restate value, reduce friction, make the action unmissable

Cut any section that does not answer a real visitor question or address a real objection.

### Design Principles for Conversion

Visual hierarchy: one dominant element per screen. Headlines should obviously be the largest element.
Whitespace: more than feels comfortable. Dense copy signals effort; whitespace signals confidence.
CTA contrast: the CTA button should be the highest-contrast element on the page.
Mobile-first: 80 percent or more of landing page visits happen on mobile. Design for thumb, not cursor.
Social proof placement: place logos or testimonials immediately after the hero, before you ask for any commitment.

### Copy Length

The right length is: long enough to answer every question a visitor needs to convert, and no longer.

Short copy works when the offer is free, low-commitment, or the visitor is already product-aware.
Long copy works when price is high, commitment is real, or objections need to be overcome.
Start longer than feels comfortable, then cut ruthlessly. Remove everything that does not earn its place.

## 4. CTAs and Conversion Mechanics

### CTA Copy

The worst CTAs are generic: "Get started," "Learn more," "Submit." The best CTAs complete the sentence "I want to ___."

Strong CTA patterns:
- Outcome-focused: "Start sending better emails"
- Specific offer: "See it in a 14-minute demo"
- Low-friction framing: "Try free for 14 days, no credit card"
- First-person voice: "Show me how it works"

Match CTA copy to the offer and the visitor's awareness stage. Product-aware visitors are ready for "Start free trial." Solution-aware visitors need "See how it works" first.

### Friction Reduction

Friction is anything that creates hesitation between intent and action. Audit these specifically:

- Is a credit card required before the visitor is ready?
- Is the CTA label ambiguous?
- Is the form longer than necessary?
- Is pricing hidden, forcing a sales call the visitor did not want?
- Are there trust signals near the CTA such as "cancel anytime," security badges, or specific testimonials?

Every friction point removed increases conversion probability.

### Social Proof Near CTAs

Place trust signals within visual proximity of your CTA. Specificity matters as much as in copy.

Weak: "Thousands of customers love us"
Strong: "4,200 marketing teams across 60 countries" or "4.7/5 on G2 based on 800 reviews"
Best: A specific result quote with name, title, and company placed directly adjacent to the CTA

### CTA Placement

- Always in the hero above the fold
- Repeat after every major value-delivering section
- Final CTA at the bottom: strongest version, most specific, most proof, most friction-reduced
- Do not make visitors scroll back up to find the action

## How to Use This Skill

Building a new page from scratch:
1. Answer the four upstream questions (who, what, why, action)
2. Determine the visitor's awareness stage
3. Load the relevant page-type reference file
4. Draft section by section using the structure guidelines
5. Apply copywriting principles throughout
6. Audit: 5 messaging layers, CTA copy, attention ratio, friction points

Reviewing or rewriting an existing page:
1. Run existing copy through the 5 messaging layers and score each one
2. Count "we/our" vs "you/your" in each section — rewrite any section where company voice dominates
3. Identify the biggest gaps (usually Differentiation and Friction)
4. Apply the copy don'ts checklist
5. Check CTA copy and placement
6. Assess attention ratio: count exits vs. the intended action
