# Article Craft

Editorial rules for long-form web articles: blog posts, thought-leadership pieces, comparisons, how-tos, listicles — and case studies, which are argument pieces with a customer story as the evidence. Assembled from the Article Generator project's section-structure and journalistic-sourcing skills plus its accumulated editor-memory (hooks, structure, closes, rejected patterns).

Scope note: voice- and sentence-level rules live in the Avoid AI Writing, Vocabulary Blacklist, and Human Writing skills; paragraph mechanics and scannability live in the Internet Writing Style Guide. This skill covers what those don't: openings, headlines, section structure, headers, TL;DR placement, closes, sourcing/anchoring, and case-study shape.

---

## Openings

The first sentence earns the second; the opening earns the rest of the article. The first two paragraphs are the most-read paragraphs in the piece (NN/g eye-tracking research) — the claim must land inside them.

**Opener patterns that work:**

- **Contrarian-claim opener.** Names the consensus, states the article disagrees. *"Most published comparisons of these two platforms hedge with the same answer: both have strengths, choose based on your needs. They are wrong."*
- **Named-stat opener.** A specific number that frames the stake, with the source identified. *"82% of agents have integrated AI tools, while 46% report no noticeable impact."*
- **In-medias-res opener.** Drops the reader inside the situation. *"You probably already bought an AI tool."*
- **Direct-claim opener.** States the article's thesis in the first sentence with no setup. *"Mailchimp is the better default. Constant Contact wins in two specific situations."*
- **Named-quote-as-opener.** Opens with a sourced quote that performs the article's argument. Use sparingly; the quote has to be doing real work.

**Banned opener types:**

- **Cinematic / scene-painting openers.** *"It's 3pm on a Tuesday in late April. The campaign brief is due Thursday at 2..."* AI scene-painting; the article warms up before getting to the claim. Same problem as "Imagine this:" or "Picture a world where" as the literal opening. (Hypothetical scenarios with explicit signaling are fine inside the article; not as the opener.)
- **Throat-clearing openers.** *"In today's competitive email marketing landscape..."* / *"In recent years, X has become increasingly important..."* / *"At its core, [topic] is about..."*
- **"In this article we'll cover..."** The reader can see the subheads. Get into the content.
- **Definitional openers** where the audience already knows the category. *"What is email marketing?"* in a platform comparison is wasted real estate.

**Edit check:** if the draft's opening doesn't give the reader a reason to keep reading by sentence two — a stake, a tension, a surprising claim, or a concrete situation — rewrite it into one of the working patterns using the draft's own strongest material.

## Headlines

- **State what the article argues, not what it covers.** *"Mailchimp vs Constant Contact: the default answer is wrong for newsletter senders"* beats *"Mailchimp vs Constant Contact: a comparison."*
- **Name the load-bearing decision factor when there is one.** *"How to build an AI marketing workflow that runs between client calls"* names the constraint.
- **Front-load the information-carrying words.** F-pattern eye tracking shows readers fixate on the first few words; bury the payload at the end and scanners miss it.
- **Sentence case, not title case** — for the title (H1), section headers (H2), and subheads (H3). Proper nouns stay capitalized. Title case reads as marketing-department register; sentence case reads as a writer talking.

## Section structure: BLUF is the default

The biggest structural failure mode in long-form drafts is a universal section template — every section the same shape reads as info collection, not argument. Choose structure per section. Three options; BLUF is the default.

### BLUF (Bottom Line Up Front) — default

The **literal first sentence** of the section states the section's bottom line as a claim. Not "open with the takeaway" loosely interpreted — the literal sentence. If the H2 is "The curve has changed materially since mid-2024," the first sentence answers it directly: *"The curve flattened across mid-2024 because the AI builder rolled out, and most reviews predate the change."*

The common drift: a setup paragraph that *describes* the topic before stating the section's claim. Setup-then-claim reads as warm-up; claim-then-evidence reads as argument. When editing, if a section's claim first appears in sentence three or later, move it to sentence one and let the former opening become evidence.

Section shape: first sentence is the claim → body is evidence, named-source quotes, supporting sub-claims → optional close (pivot, quote, or natural landing).

**The section-opener rule:** the header itself is the preview — there is no meta-prose. *"This section covers..."*, *"Below, we'll walk through..."*, *"In what follows..."* are banned. The BLUF is the opener.

### Setup-shift-how-why-expert (the arc) — at most 1–2 sections per article

Five beats: the reader's current state in concrete detail → what changes with a different approach → the procedure → why each step works → a named voice confirming. Use it only for the section where the reader's instinct is wrong and they need to *feel* the wrongness before the prescription lands. When every section follows the arc, the article reads templated. More than two arc sections in one piece is a structural failure — restructure.

### Compressed-listicle-item — for per-item slots in list pieces

Uniform shape across items so the reader can scan in parallel: item name → one-sentence verdict → the specific capability, price, or feature that justifies it → two short labeled blocks (*Best for:* / *Skip if:*). The framing sections of a listicle (intro, selection criteria, conclusion) use BLUF.

### Fact clusters need a stated takeaway

When a section presents a cluster of facts (numbers, dates, comparison data), the section must state what conclusion the reader should draw — either BLUF (claim first, facts as evidence) or cluster-then-interpret (facts first, then one interpretive sentence naming the takeaway). A list of facts with no interpretive sentence is data dumped on the reader, not evidence marshaled for an argument.

## Headers

- **Headers carry information, not labels.** *"Why most teams measure the wrong thing"* beats *"Measurement."* *"Starting price (10K contacts)"* beats *"Pricing."*
- **Subheads tell the story.** A reader who reads only the subheads should understand the arc of the piece.
- **One subhead roughly every 300 words.** Longer sections need visual breaks.
- **Subheads are one clause, not two joined by a colon.** *"Pricing at scale: Mailchimp wins above 5,000 contacts"* → *"Mailchimp wins above 5,000 contacts as the gap widens."* The colon form looks like a claim but reads as label-then-payoff staging.
- **No truth-qualifiers in headings.** "Actually," "real," "genuinely" in a heading flags defensiveness about a claim that should stand alone. *"The four things that actually need to move"* → *"The four things that need to move."* Banned in headings without exception.

## TL;DR / verdict block

- **Required for comparisons, listicles, and trend pieces**: 2–4 sentences plus a scannable bullet list. The verdict commits — no "we'll explore the tradeoffs."
- **Optional for how-tos**: the H2s already form a scannable outline; add a TL;DR only when it compresses something the headers can't (the prerequisite, the load-bearing decision).
- **Never the literal first thing after the headline.** The reader needs the question before the answer means anything. Flow: headline → opening (~2–4 paragraphs setting up the problem) → TL;DR → first H2.
- **Comparisons: table above the fold**, after the TL;DR, before the first H2. The verdict plus the table let a scanner leave with the answer.

## Closes

**Article-level patterns that work:**

- **Route by reader situation** (comparisons especially): commit per situation, not "it depends." *"Pick Mailchimp if you're a solopreneur... Pick Constant Contact if you run a nonprofit... Pick neither if you sell online..."*
- **Return to a thread from the opening** — echo a phrase, image, or claim from the opening and close the loop.
- **Refusal to resolve** — end on the unresolved tension, hand the question back to the reader. Sparingly; for essays and trend pieces whose job is expanding the reader's frame, not delivering a verdict.

**Article-level patterns to ban:** *"In summary..." / "In conclusion..." / "Overall..." / "To sum up..."*; restate-the-whole-article closings (the reader just read it); *"The path forward..." / "By embracing X, you can..." / "The future of [X] is bright..."*

**Section-level closes** do one of three things: (1) pivot to the next section by naming the load-bearing reason it follows, (2) land a named-source quote that extends the section's argument — sparingly, it becomes a tic, or (3) end naturally on the section's strongest sentence. A forced kicker is not required.

**The kicker cap:** a 3–7 word declarative landing (*"They don't."*) is effective when sparse. No more than ~30% of paragraphs across the article may close on a clipped punchline; the absence of kickers is what makes the kickers land. And when every section ends with the same closing move (a "30-day check," a named recurring kicker), the device has become scaffolding — standardize once at article level or cut it.

## Sourcing and anchoring

**Every substantive claim earns an outside-source anchor.** Not every 400 words — every claim. If a paragraph makes three claims, each earns its anchor. There is no upper cap on density; a draft can't be over-sourced, only under-sourced. A 2,500-word piece with three anchors total is undersourced regardless of its other qualities.

**Five anchor types** (a claim can land against more than one):

- **Named-expert quote** — a specific person taking a specific position: name + role + linked source. Carries argument.
- **Verified stat** — a specific number attributable to a specific report. Carries magnitude.
- **Dated event** — a specific thing that happened at a specific named entity on a date. Carries temporality.
- **Named individual observation** — a public post (LinkedIn, X, Substack) from a named practitioner. Carries practitioner credibility.
- **Published reference** — a book, paper, or well-known post by a named author. Carries framework provenance.

**What does NOT count:** "industry experts say" / "many users report" / "studies show" / "research suggests" — collective abstractions are filler with the appearance of authority. Either the claim gets a named source or the hedge gets cut. Vendor-published numbers count only as vendor-attributable anchors and should be flagged as such, ideally paired with an independent number.

**Attribution format:** first mention is full name + role + company + linked source; later mentions are name only. Attribution verbs: *said* for past statements, *argues / writes / observes* for ongoing positions. Avoid *stated, shared, exclaimed, opines* — journalist-thesaurus register.

**Quote integration:** inline for ≤25 words; blockquote for longer quotes that deserve visual weight, with attribution beneath. Never two blockquotes in a row. **Never blockquote a sentence the author wrote** — blockquotes are reserved for sourced quotes from named humans; using them to emphasize the author's own writing is an AI tell.

**Editing constraint — do not fabricate:** when editing, never invent a quote, a stat, a source, or an attribution to fill an anchoring gap. If a claim is unanchored or leans on "studies show" filler, either rephrase it as the author's own clearly-owned position or flag it in the change rationale as needing a real source — and say what kind of anchor would fit.

## Length

Comprehensiveness is not a virtue. Reference points for where good pieces land: comparisons 2,500–4,000 words, how-tos 1,500–2,500, listicles 2,000–3,500, case studies usually under 2,000. These are descriptive, not targets. A piece running past ~5,000 words without a specific reason is the "comprehensive" instinct overriding editorial choice — recommend cuts, starting with sections whose claims repeat.

## Case-study shape

A case study is an argument piece — every rule above applies (BLUF sections, anchored claims, informative headers, committed close). Its specific shape:

- **Structure: problem → stakes → solution → results.** If results appear before the problem is felt, restructure. The reader has to want the outcome before the outcome means anything.
- **Quantify the before-state, not just the after.** Improvement is meaningless without a baseline. *"Cut reporting time in half"* needs *"from 10 hours a week."*
- **Pull the single best metric into the opening** — don't make readers dig for the payoff. The named-stat opener is usually the right pattern.
- **The customer is the hero; the product is the tool.** Edit sentences where the vendor stars in the customer's story.
- **Quotes carry emotion and judgment; narrative carries facts and numbers.** A customer quote that restates a metric is wasted — the prose can cite the metric, the quote should say what it felt like or why they'd choose it again.
- **Vendor-attribution honesty.** A customer quote confirming the vendor's reported outcome is a vendor-attributable anchor, not independent evidence. The case study can still persuade — by being specific and concrete, not by pretending to neutrality.
- **The solution section describes what changed in the customer's actual workflow**, not a feature list.
- **Close with applicability** — help the reader see themselves in the story: the routing close ("if your team looks like X, this maps to you") works well.
